# Causal abstraction and circuits: survey block (what each paper defines)

Method note. The Read tool could not open PDFs here (no pdftoppm), so text was extracted with the coordinator's script into `paper.txt` beside each `paper.pdf` under `/mnt/nw/home/e.agnew/commute-data/lit_review_pdfs/survey/<arxiv_id>/`. Page numbers below are PDF page numbers ("=== page N ===" markers in the .txt), which equal the printed page numbers except for 2106.02997 (same) and 1812.03789 (same). Quotes are copied from the extracted text; where the extraction broke a word at a line end or flattened a superscript/formula, I rejoined the word and wrote formulas in plain characters (marked [formula] when I describe rather than copy one). All eight papers exist and were downloaded.

Headline for the special question (two networks implementing the same algorithm or sharing a mechanism): none of the eight papers defines it. The nearest wordings are (a) Geiger et al. 2025 p13 and Beckers-Halpern 2019 p1, p5, which say when two causal MODELS are "consistent descriptions of the same causal situation" / "work the same way" (a relation between a low-level and a high-level model, or two abstraction levels, not between two trained networks); and (b) one hedged sentence in Mueller et al. 2025 p27 fn 28 reporting that the same components were implicated before and after fine-tuning (Prakash et al.), an experimental finding by others, not a definition. A natural derived reading (two networks are both abstracted by the same high-level model H under their own alignments) is NOT stated in any of the papers; that is the reader's inference, flagged where used.

### Geiger, Lu, Icard, Potts 2021, "Causal Abstractions of Neural Networks" (arXiv:2106.02997). Status: main text read (pp1-10), formal appendix F read (pp20-21); appendices A-E, G-H skimmed only for headings.
- **Matches the list?** Yes (NeurIPS 2021, v2).
- **Definition, in the authors' words:**
  - Informal, p3: "Informally, a high-level model is a constructive abstraction of a low-level model if there is a way to partition the variables in the low-level model where each high-level variable can be assigned to a low-level partition cell, such that there is a systematic correspondence between interventions on the low-level partition cells and interventions on the high-level variables."
  - Operational test, p5 (eq. 3 in the toy case): the hypothesis is checked by comparing the interchange-intervened causal model with the interchange-intervened network, "If this equality holds for all source and base inputs a and a', then we can conclude that, for every intervention on S1, there is an equivalent intervention on L1." For the trained models, eq. (6) p8: "This asserts a correspondence between interventions on the representations at L in network N_NLI and interventions on the variable N in the causal model C_NatLog. If it holds, then N_NLI computes the relation between the aligned phrases under the node N and uses this information to compute the relation between the premise and hypothesis."
  - Formal, Appendix F p20-21. "Definition F.6. (tau-abstraction) Fix a function tau : R_L(V_L) -> R_H(V_H), which in turn fixes omega_tau : I_L -> I_H. We say (M_H, I_H) is a tau-abstraction of (M_L, I_L) if the following hold: 1. tau is surjective. 2. omega_tau is surjective. 3. for all i_L in I_L we have tau(i_L(M_L)) = omega_tau(i_L)(M_H)." "Definition F.7 (Constructive tau-abstraction). (M_H, I_H) is a constructive tau-abstraction of (M_L, I_L) if, in addition to being a tau-abstraction, we can associate with each X_H a subset P_XH of V_L, such that the mapping tau : R(V_L) -> R(V_H) decomposes into a family of functions tau_XH : R(P_XH) -> R(X_H)." (Definitions F.1-F.5 define signature, causal model, dependence, intervention, projection; p20-21.)
  - Partial success, p8-9: "Quantifying Partial Success ... We do not expect this kind of perfect correspondence to emerge in practice for real problems"; the abstraction is claimed only "on a subset of examples S of MQNLI if and only if all examples in S form a clique" (p9), and the score reported is the size of the largest clique.
- **What counts as the thing (mechanism, algorithm, circuit):** a high-level causal model (here a natural-logic tree) whose variables are each given a causal role.
- **What counts as a network having it:** the network is a (constructive) abstraction of the high-level model under an analyst-chosen alignment: interchange interventions at the aligned neuron set L give the same output as interventions on the aligned high-level variable, on all (or the largest clique of) input pairs.
- **Two networks:** Not defined. The paper compares two networks (BERT vs BiLSTM) only by how much of the same natural-logic model each realizes (Table 1a, p8: e.g. NPObj 38.3% for BERT vs 0.7% for LSTM). That is a graded score of one network against one high-level model, run separately for each network; the paper never relates the two networks to each other.
- **Kind:** formal definition (constructive abstraction, imported from Beckers-Halpern; Appendix F says "in agreement with the definitions from Beckers and Halpern [1]") plus an operational measure (interchange success, clique size).
- **What it assumes:** a known high-level model supplied by the analyst; deterministic, finite input domain (Appendix F omits exogenous variables: "as they play no role in our deterministic setting", p20); the low-level units are sets of neurons at chosen positions and layers; every pair of inputs can be intervened on (1M ordered pairs from 1000 examples, p9).
- **Who chooses what:** the analyst chooses the high-level model, the candidate locations ("Each alignment search considers aligning the intermediate high-level variable with dozens of possible locations", p7; "In principle, any location in the network could be the right one for any causal model. Testing every hypothesis in this space would be intractable. Thus, ... we consider a restricted set", p7), and the clique criterion. Two analysts with different high-level models or location sets would get different answers; the paper's only guard is a "neighborhood" of alternative high-level models (Table 1b, p8-9) that all score lower.
- **Objection:** authors' own caveat, p9: "we only achieved partial experimental success, whereas in the addition example we assumed complete success. Crucially, this means that the following analysis will be valid only on subsets of the input space on which the abstraction relation holds". (Reader's): the "abstraction on the largest clique" notion is not an invariant of the network, since it depends on the sampled 1000 examples and on which clique is largest; best clique found is 38.3% at most, so no node is abstracted on all inputs.

### Geiger, Ibeling, Zur, Chaudhary, Chauhan, Huang, Arora, Wu, Goodman, Potts, Icard 2025, "Causal Abstraction: A Theoretical Foundation for Mechanistic Interpretability" (arXiv:2301.04709, v4 = JMLR 26, 2025). Status: partly read: pp1-9 (setup, Defs 2-11), 13-14 (Defs 24-28), 16-18 (Defs 31-36), 20-23 (Defs 41-48), 30, 32-33, 39-40, 44-45. Not read: pp10-12 and 15 (intervention-algebra proofs), 24-29 (worked example), 34-38, 41-43 (method-by-method reductions).
- **Matches the list?** Yes, but the latest arXiv version (v4, May 2025) has 11 authors, not the 2023 author list. The numbered definitions asked for are all present (Defs 2-5 signature/setting/model, 9-11 interventions, 25 exact transformation, 31 alignment, 33 constructive abstraction, 41 approximate transformation, 44-48 interchange interventions).
- **Definition, in the authors' words:**
  - Def 5, p7: "A (deterministic) causal model is a pair M = (Sigma, {F_X} for X in V), such that Sigma is a signature and {F_X} is a set of mechanisms, with F_X : Val_V -> Val_X assigning a value to X as a function of the values of all the variables, including X itself."
  - Def 9, p8: "Define a hard intervention to be a partial setting i in Val_I for finite I subset V. Given a model M with signature Sigma, define M_i to be just like M, except that we replace F_X with the constant function v -> Proj_X(i) for each X in I."
  - Def 11, p8: "We define an interventional on some finite set of variables X subset V to be a family of functions I = {I_X} where I_X : Soft_X -> Soft_X for each X in X. We define M_I to be just like M, except that we replace each function F_X with the function I_X<F_X>."
  - Def 25 (exact transformation), p13: "Let M, M* be causal models and let (Psi, o) and (Psi*, o) be two intervention algebras where Psi and Psi* are interventionals on M and M*, respectively. Furthermore, let tau : Val_V -> Val_V* and omega : Psi -> Psi* be two partial surjective functions where omega is <=-preserving; that is, omega(I) <= omega(I') whenever I <= I'. Then M* is an exact transformation of M under (tau, omega) if for all I in Domain(omega), the following diagram commutes ... In a single equation: tau(Solve(M_I)) = Solve(M*_omega(I))." and p14: "This definition captures the intuitive idea that M and M* are consistent descriptions of the same causal situation."
  - Def 28 (bijective translation), p14: tau a bijection on total settings; tau(M) has mechanisms F*_X*(v*) = Proj_X*(tau(F_V(tau^-1(v*)))).
  - Def 31 (alignment), p16: "An alignment between signatures Sigma_L and Sigma_H is given by a pair <Pi, pi> of a partition Pi = {Pi_XH} for XH in V_H union {bottom} and a family pi = {pi_XH} of maps, such that: 1. The partition Pi of V_L consists of non-overlapping, non-empty cells Pi_XH subset V_L for each XH in V_H, in addition to a (possibly empty) cell Pi_bottom; 2. There is a partial surjective map pi_XH : Val_PiXH -> Val_XH for each XH in V_H."
  - Def 33, p17: "We say that H is a constructive abstraction of L under an alignment <Pi, pi> iff H is an exact transformation of L under (tau^pi, omega^pi)."
  - Def 41 (approximate transformation), p20: needs "1. a ('distance') function Sim ..., 2. a probability distribution P over Domain(omega) used to describe which interventionals are expected, 3. a real-valued statistic S" and gives the metric S_{I~P}[Sim(tau(Solve(M_I)), Solve(M*_omega(I)))]; "If this number is above a particular cutoff eta, we can say that M* is an eta-approximate abstraction of M."
  - Def 44 (interchange intervention), p21: for source inputs s1..sk and disjoint target variable sets X1..Xk, the hard intervention that sets each Xj to the value it takes when M is run on sj. Def 46 (distributed), p22: apply a bijective translation, interchange in the new variable space, translate back.
  - Def 48 (IIA), p23: "Geiger et al. (2022b) propose interchange intervention accuracy, which is simply the proportion of interchange interventions where the low-level and high-level causal models have the same input-output behavior".
  - Circuits, Section 3.5.2, p39: "Sub-circuit analysis ... aims to identify a minimal circuit C subset V x V between components in a model L that is sufficient to perform particular behavior that we represent with a partial function B ... the behavior B should remain intact when all connections between model components ... are ablated." p40: "The high-level causal model H is an exact transformation of the low-level neural model L under (tau, omega) exactly when the subcircuit C preserves the behavior B."
  - Graded faithfulness, p32: "Crucially, faithfulness should be a graded notion ... but precisely which metric of faithfulness is correct will depend on the situation."
- **What counts as the thing (mechanism, algorithm, circuit):** the high-level causal model H (the algorithm); a circuit is a subgraph that preserves a behaviour B under ablation (p39), itself recast as a three-variable abstraction.
- **What counts as a network having it:** H is an exact (or eta-approximate) transformation / constructive abstraction of the network under an alignment <Pi, pi>, checked by interchange interventions (IIA).
- **Two networks:** Not defined for trained networks. Closest, p13: "Researchers have been interested in the question of when two models--potentially defined on different signatures--are compatible with one another in the sense that they could both accurately describe the same target causal phenomena." and p14 "consistent descriptions of the same causal situation" for exact transformation; p14 on bijective translation: "M and M* are just two equivalent (and inter-translatable) descriptions of the same causal setup." Remark 27 (p14): exact transformations compose. These relate two causal MODELS (levels), not two separately trained networks; the paper never says a pair of networks "share" a mechanism. (Reader's inference only: two networks both abstracted by one H under their own alignments would be "the same algorithm", but this is not stated.) Section 3.3.1 p32-33 says of LIME that "two models having the same local input-output behavior is a matter of causality" but that the interpretable model "lacks any connection to the internal causal dynamics", i.e. same behaviour is explicitly not enough.
- **Kind:** formal definition (the most complete in this block), plus operational measure (IIA).
- **What it assumes:** a known high-level model H; deterministic models, "we do not incorporate probability into the picture" (p6); a chosen family of interventionals and a probability P over them; alignment partition and maps chosen or searched; for IIA, input and output variables aligned by identity.
- **Who chooses what:** the analyst chooses H, the alignment class (localist partition vs bijective translation), Sim, P and the cutoff eta. p30: "We should have the flexibility to choose the units of analysis, free of restrictive assumptions that may rule out meaningful structures. Whether a particular decomposition ... is useful for mechanistic interpretability should be understood as an empirical hypothesis that can be falsified through experimentation." p17: marginalization and value merge "relate closely to concepts identified in the philosophy of science literature as being critical to addressing the problem of variable choice". Two analysts with different H or different Sim/P/eta get different verdicts; the theory fixes none of them. Remark 47 (p22) also notes the alignment functions are induced from the partition and the input alignment only when "two different inputs" do not give the same low-level value with different high-level values; otherwise "the causal abstraction relationship simply wouldn't hold".
- **Objection:** the paper has no limitations section; the nearest statement of a limit is p33 on LIME: "Crucially, however, the interpretable model lacks any connection to the internal causal dynamics of the uninterpretable model", and p13 that exact transformation "is all-or-nothing" (p20: "This binary concept prevents us from having a graded notion of faithful interpretation"). (Reader's): the definition leaves the alignment map class and the pool of interventionals as free parameters; Sutter et al. (below) show that freeing the alignment map class makes the relation vacuous, and this paper does not constrain it beyond requiring a bijection (Def 28). No definition of equality of mechanism across two models.

### Geiger, Wu, Potts, Icard, Goodman 2024 (arXiv 2023), "Finding Alignments Between Interpretable Causal Variables and Distributed Neural Representations" (arXiv:2303.02536, CLeaR 2024). Status: partly read: pp1-8, 10-13, 22-23 (FAQ appendix). Not read: p9 and the experiment details of pp9-10 beyond the discussion, appendix pp14-21.
- **Matches the list?** Yes. Published as CLeaR 2024 (PMLR 236), v4 of Feb 2024; "Geiger, Wu, Potts, Icard, Goodman" matches.
- **Definition, in the authors' words:**
  - p4, Def 1: "A high-level causal model H is a constructive abstraction of a low-level causal model L under alignment Pi exactly when the following holds for every low-level input setting x and low-level intervention I <- i: tau(L_{I<-i}(x)) = H_{tau(I<-i)}(tau(x))". Followed by: "H being a causal abstraction of L under Pi guarantees that the causal mechanism for each high-level variable X is a faithful rendering of the causal mechanisms for the low-level variables in Pi_X."
  - p3: "In causal abstraction analysis, we ask whether a specific low-level model like N implements a high-level algorithm like B. This is always relative to a specific alignment of variables between the two models. An alignment Pi = ({Pi_X}, {tau_X}) assigns to each high-level variable X a set of low-level variables Pi_X and a function tau_X that maps from values of the low-level variables in Pi_X to values of the aligned high-level variable X."
  - p6-7, Def 3 (distributed interchange intervention): rotate a set of target units with an invertible map R into a space with orthogonal subspaces, swap subspaces with the source's values, rotate back. p7-8, Def 4: the DAS objective is cross-entropy between the high-level model's counterfactual output and the network's, with "the low-level and high-level models ... frozen during learning so we are only changing the alignment" (p7).
  - Graded notion, p1 and p23: "the proportion of interchange interventions with the same effect (interchange intervention accuracy; IIA) provides a graded notion"; p23: "When IIA is alpha < 100%, we rely on the graded notion of alpha-on-average approximate causal abstraction".
- **What counts as the thing (mechanism, algorithm, circuit):** a high-level algorithm B (e.g. hierarchical equality, lexical entailment) with intermediate variables; the "thing" is the intermediate variable realized in a subspace.
- **What counts as a network having it:** 100% (or high) IIA for some rotation R (the alignment). p2: "we find a distributed alignment with 100% IIA. In other words, the network is perfectly abstracted by the high-level model; the distinction between the learned neural model and the symbolic algorithm is thus one of implementation."
- **Two networks:** Not defined, not discussed. Only one fine-tuned network (or one MLP) per high-level model is analysed. The only multi-model comparison is across alternative HIGH-LEVEL models for one network (p10: "the relative IIA rankings between the high-level models also show which high-level model is a better approximation", p23).
- **Kind:** formal definition (restated from Geiger et al. 2025) plus operational method (DAS) plus experimental findings.
- **What it assumes:** p22-23 (FAQ): "We assume a partial or complete causal graph of the data generation process. Specifically, we assume to have interchangeable high-level variables defined for the causal graph. Additionally, we assume we can sample counterfactual data (i.e., base and source inputs where they differ in values of high-level variables) based on the causal graph." Orthogonal (linear) rotation: "an orthogonal matrix assumes a linear transformation before aligning with a high-level variable, which may not be optimal if the aligning variable is represented in a non-linear sub-manifold" (p22).
- **Who chooses what:** analyst picks H, the target layer and positions, the subspace sizes ("we still have discrete hyperparameters (N, |Y0|, ..., |Yk|)", p8), the random seed (best of three runs reported: "we use random seeds and pick the best results out of our distinct runs", p22). The paper itself notes alignment choice can flip the verdict: p5 "Since the choice of basis was arbitrary, our negative conclusion about the causal abstraction relation was spurious." Two analysts with different H or subspace sizes can get different IIA.
- **Objection:** authors' own caveat, p22: the orthogonal-matrix choice "assumes a linear transformation ... In practice, this transformation is computationally difficult to find ... We leave these investigations to future works." and, p13, "This highlights the need to investigate the causal substructure of neural representations" (the lexical-entailment variable at 100% IIA decomposes into two word-identity subspaces, so a 100% IIA match does not show the variable is stored as the high-level model says). (Reader's): IIA is reported on training data and best-of-seeds (Table 2 caption p12 "on training data"); no random-network control in this paper (Sutter et al. add one).

### Sutter, Minder, Hofmann, Pimentel 2025, "The Non-Linear Representation Dilemma: Is Causal Abstraction Enough for Mechanistic Interpretability?" (arXiv:2507.08802, NeurIPS 2025). Status: partly read: pp1-7, 9-10. Not read: p8 (experimental results text beyond one paragraph on p9) and appendices pp11-42 (proofs).
- **Matches the list?** Yes (v2, Nov 2025).
- **Definition, in the authors' words:** they restate the Beckers-Halpern definitions for network N and algorithm A.
  - p4, Def 1 (from Beckers and Halpern 2019): "An algorithm A is a tau-abstraction of a neural network N iff: tau is surjective; I_A = omega_tau(I_N); and there exists a surjective tau_eta_x such that: for all x in X and I_N in I_N: tau(f_N^{psi_int}(x, I_N)) = f_A^{:eta_int}(tau_eta_x(x), I_A) where I_A = omega_tau(I_N)". p4, "A is a strong tau-abstraction of N if it is a tau-abstraction and I_A is maximal, meaning that any intervention is allowed on algorithm A."
  - p4, Def 2: "An algorithm A is a constructive abstraction of a neural network N iff there exists an tau : for which A is a strong tau-abstraction of N; and tau is constructive." (tau is blockwise over a partition of neurons.) Added constraint p4: input layer maps by identity, output by argmax.
  - p5, Def 6: "An algorithm A is a distributed abstraction of a neural network N iff there exists an tau : for which A is a strong tau-abstraction of N; and tau is a distributed abstraction map." Def 7 (input-restricted distributed abstraction, "inspired by Geiger et al., 2024b") is "the notion typically used in practice by machine learning practitioners" (p5).
  - Main result, p6, Theorem 1: "Given any algorithm A and any neural network N such that Assumps. 1 to 5 hold, we can show that A is an input-restricted distributed abstraction of N." Assumptions (p6): countable input space; input-injective hidden layers; strict output-surjectivity in all layers; matchable partial orderings; "the DNN N solves the given task T."
  - Abstract, p1: "we prove that under reasonable assumptions, any neural network can be mapped to any algorithm, rendering this unrestricted notion of causal abstraction trivial and uninformative."
- **What counts as the thing (mechanism, algorithm, circuit):** an algorithm A = a deterministic causal DAG with input, inner, output nodes, computing the task (p2-3, "we term each such hypothesis an algorithm").
- **What counts as a network having it:** A is an (input-restricted) distributed abstraction of N under an alignment map phi; the paper's point is that this is vacuous unless phi is restricted (identity map = privileged bases, orthogonal linear map = linear representation hypothesis, RevNet = non-linear).
- **Two networks:** Not defined. Footnote 4 p4 notes "algorithm A and network N must have the same outputs on the input set X". Experiments compare randomly initialised, partly trained and fully trained models (Pythia checkpoints, p9: "as training advances, even less complex alignment maps ... eventually attain perfect alignment") but each against an algorithm, not against each other. p1 cites Meloux et al. 2025 for "many algorithms can be aligned to the same neural network" (many algorithms, one network: the reverse of the special question).
- **Kind:** formal result about the formal definitions (a theorem), plus experimental findings.
- **What it assumes:** the five assumptions above; the alignment map family is the thing varied.
- **Who chooses what:** the analyst chooses the alignment-map family (identity / orthogonal / RevNet with L_rn layers and width d_rn), the partition and intervention sizes (p6: "one must assume a partitioning ... which remains fixed during the method's application"). The paper's conclusion p10: "If you have reason to believe the linear representation hypothesis holds for the features you wish to extract, constraining phi to linear functions may be advised. If you do not, however, you may face the non-linear representation dilemma". So two analysts with different alignment families can get "perfect" and "no" abstraction respectively; no principled way to choose.
- **Objection:** authors' stated limitations, p10: "Our proof that any algorithm can be aligned with any DNN (Theorem 1) relies on a form of overfitting. Yet, our experiments show that the learned alignment maps phi generalise to unseen test data ... Further, our theorem relies on two strong assumptions: input-injectivity (Assump. 2) and strict output-surjectivity (Assump. 3) in all layers." Also p10: "This existence proof ... requires perfect information about both and thus representing a form of extreme overfitting. Crucially, this theorem offers no guarantees regarding the learnability of the alignment map phi from limited data or its generalisation to unseen inputs." (Reader's): this is the strongest objection for our use. Any definition of "reuse" that relies on an alignment map between a model and an algorithm (or between two models) inherits the vacuity unless the map class is fixed in advance and a random-weights control is run.

### Makelov, Lange, Nanda 2023, "Is This the Subspace You Are Looking for? An Interpretability Illusion for Subspace Activation Patching" (arXiv:2311.17030). Status: partly read: pp1-8, 17-18, 25-27. Not read: pp9-16 (IOI illusion experiments), 19-24, 28+ (factual recall, appendices).
- **Matches the list?** Yes; title in full is as above (the list gave the first clause only). Not a formal-definition paper: it is an experimental/theoretical illusion result.
- **Definition, in the authors' words:** none formal. Closest, the informal faithfulness requirement, p7: "What would make activation patching a good attribution method? Intuitively, an equivalence is needed: an activation patch should work if and only if the component/subspace being patched is indeed a faithful to the model's computation representation of the concept we seek to localize." Two properties are needed for a patch to change the output in the intended way (p7): "correlation with the concept: v must be activated differently by the two prompts" and "potential for changing model outputs: v must be 'causally connected' to the model's outputs". The illusion (p8): a direction v_illusory = (v_disconnected + v_dormant)/sqrt 2 gets the first property from a direction in the nullspace of downstream components and the second from a dormant direction not activated differently by the two prompts. Success-case criterion, p17: the directions possess "the necessary and sufficient properties of a successful activation patch -- being both correlated with input variation and causal for the targeted behavior -- in a way that does not rely on a large causally disconnected component for the effect."
- **What counts as the thing (mechanism, algorithm, circuit):** a circuit = "collections of narrow, task-specific algorithms" whose operations are "atomic building blocks akin to variables in a computer program (Olah, 2022) or causal model" (p2); the thing tested here is a 1-D feature subspace.
- **What counts as a network having it:** a patch along the subspace reproduces the output change AND passes extra mechanistic checks (removing the causally disconnected component does not kill the effect; the direction aligns with the direction downstream heads read, cosine about 0.78 with v_grad, p18).
- **Two networks:** Not discussed; all analysis is on one model (GPT-2 small; GPT-2 XL for facts).
- **Kind:** experimental finding (with a distilled mathematical example), with informal criteria for faithfulness.
- **What it assumes:** the linear representation hypothesis for subspaces; that model components (heads vs MLP) are meaningful boundaries; a "ground truth" circuit from Wang et al. as reference for IOI.
- **Who chooses what:** the analyst picks the component, the layer, the subspace dimension, the method (DAS vs gradient direction). The authors note p26: "For an explanation to be 'illusory', there has to be some notion of what the 'true' explanation is. We admit that a definition of a 'ground truth' mechanistic explanation is conceptually challenging." So their verdict itself rests on the analyst's choice of units.
- **Objection:** authors' own, p26: "The importance of correct model units. A further implicit assumption in our work is that model components are meaningful boundaries for mechanistic explanation. As we illustrate in Appendix A.3, our toy example of the illusion can be considered in a rotated basis, in which the 'illusory' direction appears 'meaningful'." (Reader's): this says a high interchange effect on a subspace is not evidence that the subspace is what the network uses; for a reuse definition, "same subspace gives the same patch effect before and after fine-tuning" could be satisfied by a dormant pathway.

### Beckers, Halpern 2019, "Abstracting Causal Models" (arXiv:1812.03789, AAAI 2019). Status: main text read (pp1-8); proofs (p9 and appendix) not read.
- **Matches the list?** Yes (v4).
- **Definition, in the authors' words:**
  - p4, Def 3.1 (from Rubenstein et al., "RW+"): exact (tau-omega)-transformation: with omega "an order-preserving, surjective mapping" on interventions and tau a map on states, "for every intervention Y<-y in I_L, we have Pr_H^{omega(Y<-y)} = tau(Pr_L^{Y<-y})". They explain: "Roughly speaking, it says that if you start from the low-level intervention Y<-y and move up to the high-level model following two distinct routes, you end up at the same place." (p3).
  - p4, Def 3.5 (uniform transformation): "M_H is a uniform (tau-omega)-transformation of M_L if, for all Pr_L, there exists Pr_H such that (M_H, Pr_H) is an exact (tau-omega)-transformation of (M_L, Pr_L)."
  - p6, Def 3.12 and 3.13: omega_tau is induced from tau: "omega_tau(X<-x) = Y<-y if Y subset V_H, y in R_H(Y), and tau(Rst(V_L, x)) = Rst(V_H, y)". "(M_H, I_H) is a tau-abstraction of (M_L, I_L) if the following conditions hold: tau is surjective; there is a surjective function tau_U : R_L(U_L) -> R_H(U_H) compatible with tau; I_H = omega_tau(I_L)."
  - p6, Def 3.15: "M_H is a strong tau-abstraction of M_L if I_H^tau = I_H^*, the set of all high-level interventions, and (M_H, I_H^tau) is a tau-abstraction of (M_L, I_L^tau)."
  - p7, Def 3.19: "M_H is a constructive tau-abstraction of M_L if M_H is a strong tau-abstraction of M_L and, if V_H = {Y1, ..., Yn}, then there exists a partition P = {Z1, ..., Z_{n+1}} of V_L, where Z1..Zn are nonempty, and mappings tau_i : R_L(Z_i) -> R_H(Y_i) for i = 1..n such that tau = (tau_1, ..., tau_n)".
- **What counts as the thing (mechanism, algorithm, circuit):** a high-level causal model M_H that is a "higher-level description" of M_L. Not specific to neural networks (examples: voters, kinetic energy, pixels).
- **What counts as a network having it:** (the paper has no networks) a low-level model has the high-level model as an abstraction iff the commuting condition holds for the interventions the map induces.
- **Two networks:** Not defined for two lower-level models. The nearest wording concerns when two models should NOT count as the same. p1: "Abstraction almost by definition involves ignoring inessential differences. So it seems that RW+ would want to claim that if there exists an exact transformation from M1 and M2, then M2 and M1 are the same, except for 'inessential differences'. This leads to the obvious question: what counts as an inessential difference? Of course, this is to some extent in the eye of the beholder, and may well depend on the application." And p4 Example 3.4: two completely unrelated models are exact transformations of each other, "suggests to us that exact transformations are not capturing the essence of abstraction"; p5 Example 3.10: "we would expect that if M_L and M_H are different, then we should not have M_L and M_H being abstractions of each other." p5: "M1 and M2 do, in a sense, work the same way as far as these allowed interventions go."
- **Kind:** formal definition (a hierarchy of definitions).
- **What it assumes:** recursive (acyclic) causal models; a given set of allowed interventions or, for strong abstraction, all interventions; the map tau supplied.
- **Who chooses what:** the analyst gives tau (and, in the weak notions, the allowed interventions I_L). p1: "what counts as an inessential difference ... is to some extent in the eye of the beholder, and may well depend on the application." p8 (conclusion): "More work needs to be done to understand which abstraction relation is most suitable for a given application." So two analysts choosing different tau or different allowed interventions get different verdicts; the definitions only rule out some degenerate cases.
- **Objection:** authors' own (p7-8): constructive abstraction is "the cleanest", but "there exist applications for which the weaker abstraction relations are more appropriate", and the approximate version is deferred: "First, we hope to generalize the abstraction relation to a notion of approximate abstraction, given that in most real-life settings the mappings between different levels are only approximately correct." (Reader's): the definition is exact and all-or-nothing, so no trained network will satisfy it; and it is silent on how to pick tau, the very step the later neural-network papers show is free enough to make the relation vacuous (Sutter et al.).

### Wang, Variengien, Conmy, Shlegeris, Steinhardt 2022, "Interpretability in the Wild: a Circuit for Indirect Object Identification in GPT-2 Small" (arXiv:2211.00593, v1 Nov 2022, ICLR 2023). Status: partly read: pp1-3, 9-12. Not read: pp4-8 (circuit discovery by path patching), appendices.
- **Matches the list?** Yes; the criteria faithfulness, completeness, minimality are in Section 4 (pp9-11).
- **Definition, in the authors' words:**
  - Circuit, p3: "If we think of a model as a computational graph M where nodes are terms in its forward pass (neurons, attention heads, embeddings, etc.) and edges are the interactions between those terms (residual connections, attention, projections, etc.), a circuit C is a subgraph of M responsible for some behavior (such as completing the IOI task)." Function of a circuit via knockouts, p3: "C(x) is defined by knocking out all nodes in M\C and taking the resulting logit outputs in the modified computational graph"; knockout is mean ablation (p3).
  - Performance measure, p9: "F(C) = E_{X~pIOI}[f(C(X); X)]" with f the logit difference between the indirect-object and subject names.
  - Faithfulness, p9: "The first one, faithfulness, asks that C has similar performance to the full model M, as measured by |F(M) - F(C)|." (Result: 0.46, i.e. 13% of F(M)=3.56.)
  - Completeness, p10: "we introduce the completeness criterion: for every subset K subset C, the incompleteness score |F(C \ K) - F(M \ K)| should be small. In other words, C and M should not just be similar, but remain similar under knockouts."
  - Minimality, p10: "Formally, the minimality criterion is whether for every node v in C there exists a subset K subset C \ {v} that has high minimality score |F(C \ (K union {v})) - F(C \ K)|."
  - Summary, p2: "faithfulness (the circuit can perform the task as well as the whole model), completeness (the circuit contains all the nodes used to perform the task), and minimality (the circuit doesn't contain nodes irrelevant to the task)."
- **What counts as the thing (mechanism, algorithm, circuit):** a subgraph of the model's component graph (26 attention heads in 7 classes) plus a human-readable algorithm ("of the two names in the sentence, predict the name that isn't the subject of the last clause", p2).
- **What counts as a network having it:** the model's own graph contains the circuit; it passes the three criteria on a task distribution pIOI (quantitative thresholds are not fixed; "small"/"high" are left open).
- **Two networks:** Not defined. One sentence on a second model, p12: "As a starting point, we performed a preliminary analysis of GPT-2 medium. We found that this model also has a sparse set of heads directly influencing the logits. However, not all of these heads attend to IO and S, suggesting more complex behavior than the Name Movers Heads in GPT-2 small." This is an observation, with no criterion for "same circuit" across models.
- **Kind:** operational measures only (three criteria, defined as formulae but with no pass thresholds) plus an experimental finding (the circuit).
- **What it assumes:** a task distribution pIOI (15 templates), a chosen metric (logit difference), a choice of ablation (mean over a reference distribution), a choice of component granularity (attention heads, not features), and a hand-built high-level story. No explicit high-level causal model with an alignment map.
- **Who chooses what:** the analyst chooses the task, distribution, metric, ablation type, node granularity, which K subsets to search (completeness is searched by three sampling methods; the third, greedy, found high incompleteness up to 3.09, 87% of logit difference, p10, "usually not semantically interpretable"), and what counts as "small" or "high". Two analysts could well produce different circuits for the same behaviour; the paper itself found backup heads that appear only after ablation (p9, Section 3.4).
- **Objection:** authors' own, p12: "However, our work also has several limitations. First, despite the detailed analysis presented here, we do not understand several components. Those include the attention patterns of the S-Inhibition Heads, and the effect of MLPs and layer norms." and p9: "Yet Section 3.4 shows that our experimental methods do not fully account for all name-moving in the model: some new heads take on this role after the main Name Mover Heads are knocked out." and the circuit "fails to pass the most challenging tests" (p2). (Reader's): the criteria are conditional on ablation type and distribution; redundant (backup) mechanisms mean "which nodes the model uses" depends on which nodes are knocked out, which is directly relevant to fine-tuning, where a backup path could be promoted.

### Mueller et al. 2025, "The Quest for the Right Mediator: Surveying Mechanistic Interpretability Through the Lens of Causal Mediation Analysis" (arXiv:2408.01416, v3 Sept 2025, Computational Linguistics). Status: partly read: pp1-8 (definitions and criteria), 26-28 (discussion). Not read: pp9-25 (history, mediator types, search methods), 29+.
- **Matches the list?** Partly. The arXiv id and first author match, but the v3 title is "The Quest for the Right Mediator: Surveying Mechanistic Interpretability Through the Lens of Causal Mediation Analysis", not the subtitle I recall from v1 ("A History, Survey, and Theoretical Grounding of Causal Interpretability"); it is a survey, not a new definition paper.
- **Definition, in the authors' words:**
  - p5: "In the causality literature, a mechanism is defined as a causal chain from cause X to effect Y (Salmon 1984; Pearl 2009). The mechanistic interpretability literature, while closely related to causal interpretability, does not enforce this causally grounded definition of mechanism". And: "We believe that the causal definition of 'mechanism' is an actionable one that makes the main challenge of mechanistic interpretability more precise--to reverse-engineer algorithmic understanding or control of model behaviors, where 'algorithm' is equivalent to a task-specific causal graph H explaining how the model C performs a given task."
  - p4-5: "The main challenge of mechanistic interpretability studies, then, is to define a mapping from components Z in computation graph C to a high-level causal graph H consisting of nodes and edges (V, E) that explains how the model performs some specific behavior."
  - Indirect effect, p6: IE(m; X, x; V_i, v_i, v'_i) = m(V_i = v_i | X = x) - m(do(V_i = v'_i) | X = x).
  - Criteria, p7: "the ideal causal mediator is one that achieves a Pareto optimum between maximal sparsity (using as little of the computation graph as possible), generality (explaining the widest breadth of data), selectivity (explaining only the phenomenon of interest and as little else as possible), and faithfulness (retaining fidelity to the original model)." And p7 for hypothesis verification: "counterfactual faithfulness ... measures whether the model's behavior changes in the expected manner when we perform counterfactual interventions to a specific part of the hypothesized mechanism; this is typically quantified as the interchange intervention accuracy (IIA; Geiger et al. 2021)."
- **What counts as the thing (mechanism, algorithm, circuit):** an "algorithm" is "a task-specific causal graph H"; a mechanism is a causal chain; mediators are the nodes (neurons, heads, directions, SAE features).
- **What counts as a network having it:** the network's computation graph C contains mediators Z mapped onto nodes of H and the mapping scores well on sparsity, generality, selectivity and faithfulness; no threshold.
- **Two networks:** Not defined. One mention of a before/after-fine-tuning comparison, p27, footnote 28, attached to the remark that non-basis-aligned directions may need rediscovery after fine-tuning: "Though Prakash et al. (2024) find that the same model components are implicated in an entity tracking task before and after fine-tuning." (Prakash et al. is not in this block and was not read; this is only the survey's one-line report of it.) The survey also lists "model editing" and "parameter-efficient model adaptation" as localization uses (p8).
- **Kind:** informal definition / taxonomy and evaluation criteria (a perspective); not a formal definition.
- **What it assumes:** that the analyst can name a task and a candidate H; that interventions are counterfactual replacements with a chosen counterfactual value (p6 fn 8: "The choice of counterfactual v'_i significantly affects which components will be identified as influential").
- **Who chooses what:** the paper's thesis is that the mediator type is the analyst's choice and that "the best mediator will therefore depend on one's goals" (p26); the intervention value, task and metric are also chosen (p6 fn 8). p27 on alignment search: "The primary downside is that we must anticipate the mechanisms that models employ to perform a task, as demonstrated in Appendix C; if we cannot anticipate them, then curating data and refining one's causal hypotheses may require significant human effort." Two analysts could pick different mediators and get different graphs.
- **Objection:** authors' own, p27: alignment-search methods "are subject to the same confounds as any other optimization-based technique: the module we are optimizing could directly learn the phenomenon, rather than extracting it from the model (Hewitt and Liang 2019; Sutter et al. 2025)." and, p1, the motivating complaint: "there is little unity in the field: most studies employ ad-hoc evaluations and do not share theoretical foundations". (Reader's): no pass threshold is given for any of the four criteria, so a verdict "this mechanism is reused" cannot be read off from them.

## Cross-paper notes for the special question and the analyst-choice question (reader's, from the above)
- Only Geiger et al. 2025 (pp13-14), Beckers-Halpern (pp1, 4-5) and, loosely, Mueller et al. (p27 fn 28) say anything about two things being "the same". All concern two causal models at different levels, or a before/after comparison reported from outside the block. No paper defines two trained networks sharing a mechanism.
- Every formal route to "network implements algorithm" needs (1) a high-level model H, (2) an alignment map family, (3) an intervention distribution and a similarity threshold. None of the papers fixes any of the three; Sutter et al. p6/p10 show that leaving (2) free makes the relation vacuous, Makelov et al. p26 show that a high patch effect can come from a causally disconnected subspace, and Wang et al. p10 show the answer depends on which subsets are knocked out.
- Wang et al.'s criteria and the DAS/IIA measure are the two operational scores available; neither yields a pass/fail without a chosen threshold.
