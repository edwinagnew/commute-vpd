### Raghu, Raghu, Bengio, Vinyals 2020, "Rapid learning or feature reuse? Towards understanding the effectiveness of MAML" (arXiv:1909.09157). Status: main text read (pp. 1-10; appendix pp. 13+ not read)
- **Matches the list?** Yes (ICLR 2020 version). Note it is about meta-learning (MAML inner-loop adaptation), not ordinary transfer or fine-tuning of a pretrained model; the full title adds "Towards understanding the effectiveness of MAML".
- **Definition, in the authors' words:** p.2-3: "In rapid learning, large representational and parameter changes occur during adaptation to each new task as a result of favorable weight conditioning from the meta-initialization. In feature reuse, the meta-initialization already contains highly useful features that can mostly be reused as is for new tasks, so little task-specific adaptation occurs." Also p.1 abstract: "feature reuse, with the meta-initialization already containing high quality features". And p.10 for model-based methods: "This mode of operation is purely feature reuse – we do not use information defining the task to directly influence the decision function."
- **What counts as the thing:** the latent representation (activations) of each layer of the network body (all layers except the final head); "the latent functions" p.4.
- **What counts as the original model already having it:** the meta-initialization (the weights before the inner-loop updates) "already contains" the features; operationally, test accuracy is unchanged when body layers are frozen during adaptation (p.4, Table 1) and CCA/CKA similarity of each layer's activations before vs after adaptation is near 1 (p.5, Fig. 2).
- **Kind:** operational measure only (two tests: freezing layers at test time; CCA/CKA similarity of a layer before vs after adaptation), plus an informal two-way contrast (rapid learning vs feature reuse).
- **What it assumes:** layered feed-forward network with a separable head and body; a task-specific head that is allowed to change; few-shot tasks drawn from a task distribution; a meta-trained initialization; the base model must already be good on the target tasks after head adaptation (reuse is read from "accuracy does not drop when the body is frozen").
- **Is it about the fine-tune or about the task?** Mostly about the fine-tune (before/after comparison of the same network's representations), but the freezing test is behavioural (accuracy), so two adaptations that reach the same accuracy while one moves the body and one does not would get different CCA answers but the same freezing answer. Unclear whether a fine-tune that rotates the body by an invertible map would score as reuse: CCA is invariant to invertible linear maps, so it would.
- **Objection:** Authors, p.10: "We note that our study mainly considered benchmark datasets, such as Omniglot and MiniImageNet. It is an interesting future direction to consider rapid learning and feature reuse in MAML on other few-shot learning datasets". (reader's) The headline is a binary (little change vs large change) judged by CCA/CKA on the body only; the head is excluded by construction (p.4: "the head must necessarily change for each task"), so the definition cannot say anything about reuse in the layer where the new task-specific mapping lives. (reader's) Also the p.5 result "CCA similarity scores of > 0.9" is a similarity of activations, not of the function computed downstream: high similarity does not show the downstream circuit is unchanged.

### Raghu, Zhang, Kleinberg, Bengio 2019, "Transfusion: Understanding transfer learning for medical imaging" (arXiv:1902.07208). Status: main text read (pp. 1-9; appendix not read)
- **Matches the list?** Yes (NeurIPS 2019, v3). Title in the PDF is "Transfusion: Understanding Transfer Learning for Medical Imaging".
- **Definition, in the authors' words:** no definition given. Nearest sentences: p.1 abstract: "some of the differences from transfer learning are due to the over-parametrization of standard models rather than sophisticated feature reuse. We isolate where useful feature reuse occurs". p.4: "Does feature reuse take place, and if so, where exactly?" p.7: "any serious feature reuse is restricted to the lowest couple of layers, which is where similarity before/after training is clearly higher for pretrained weights vs random initialization." p.5: they compare "the similarity of the hidden representations learned when training from pretrained weights to those learned when training from random initialization."
- **What counts as the thing:** per-layer hidden representations (activation vectors, compared by (SV)CCA, p.5) and, in the weight-transfusion experiment, the pretrained weights themselves (p.8: "transfering a contiguous set of some of the pretrained weights, randomly initializing the rest of the network").
- **What counts as the original model already having it:** operationally, a layer shows reuse if its CCA similarity to its own initialization is higher after fine-tuning when initialized from pretrained weights than when initialized randomly (Fig. 3, p.6, "the only place where similarity with initialization is significantly higher for pretrained weights"); and if copying those layers' weights speeds convergence (Fig. 7, p.8).
- **Kind:** operational measure only (CCA similarity to initialization, plus weight transfusion and convergence speed).
- **What it assumes:** layered CNN; the same architecture before and after (weights are copied layer by layer); a random-initialization control trained on the same target task; a downstream task the base model is fine-tuned on (so the base model does not already do the task).
- **Is it about the fine-tune or about the task?** About the pair (pretrained, fine-tuned) vs a random-initialization baseline, so it mixes fine-tune and task. Two fine-tunes of one model reaching the same behaviour could differ: the CCA-to-init test reads how far each moved. Authors note a confound: p.6, "larger models change much less during training, especially in the lowest layers. This is true even when they are randomly initialized, ruling out feature reuse as the sole cause", i.e. small movement is not proof of reuse.
- **Objection:** Authors (p.5, Fig. 4 caption, p.6): "Large models move less through training at lower layers" and the sentence above, which concede that "does not move" and "is reused" are not the same. No separate limitations section found. (reader's) Reuse is inferred from two proxies that the authors themselves show can disagree with each other and with convergence speed (p.7-8: "feature independent benefits", Mean Var init reproduces much of the speedup with no features); so there is no single quantity called "reuse".

### Neyshabur, Sedghi, Zhang 2020, "What is being transferred in transfer learning?" (arXiv:2008.11687). Status: main text read (pp. 1-9; appendix pp. 10+ not read)
- **Matches the list?** Yes (NeurIPS 2020, v2). Author order on the list was "Neyshabur, Sedghi, Zhang"; matches.
- **Definition, in the authors' words:** no definition of feature reuse given. Nearest sentences: p.3: "The benefits of transfer learning are generally believed to come from reusing the pre-trained feature hierarchy." p.3: "we partition the image of the downstream tasks into equal sized blocks and shuffle the blocks randomly. The shuffling disrupts high level visual features in those images but keeps the low level statistics about the pixel values intact." p.5 (on CKA results): "This emphasizes on role of feature reuse and that two P-T are reusing the same features." Their formal definitions are of a loss "basin" (Definition 3.1, p.6) and "module criticality" (Definition 3.2, p.7), not of reuse.
- **What counts as the thing:** the pretrained feature hierarchy; features are compared as layer outputs by CKA (p.5, Table 1); also the pretrained weights as a location in parameter space (l2 distance p.5; same loss basin p.6).
- **What counts as the original model already having it:** indirectly: fine-tuned model P-T has high CKA with the pretrained model P at each layer (Table 1, p.5: P-T & P vs RI-T & P), is close in l2 distance (Table 2), and lies in the same flat basin as other fine-tunes (p.6, "no performance barrier between the two instances of models trained from pre-trained weights"). Separately, a feature-reuse effect is measured as the accuracy gap P-T minus RI-T and its shrinkage under block shuffling (p.4).
- **Kind:** experimental finding only for "feature reuse" (the block-shuffle gap is an operational measure of the share of benefit due to visual features, p.4); formal definitions exist only for basin and module criticality.
- **What it assumes:** layered CNN (ResNet-50), same architecture before and after; image classification in the visual domain (p.9 "we focus on studying transfer learning in visual domain"); source and target domains differ but share an input type; a random-init control.
- **Is it about the fine-tune or about the task?** Mixed. The block-shuffle test changes the task, not the fine-tune. The CKA, l2 and basin tests are about the fine-tune: two fine-tunes of one model that gain the same behaviour could differ in distance and basin, so they could get different answers. The paper reports that two P-T models are close to each other and to P (Table 2), so these are not task-only measures.
- **Objection:** Authors, p.9: "Transfer learning in text domains is characteristically different from transfer learning in visual domains due to the nature of the data, utility of untrained representations, pre-training techniques and the amount of distribution shifts between pre-training and downstream tasks. In this paper, we focus on studying transfer learning in visual domain." Also p.7: "Whether this is related to the module moving to another basin is unclear." (reader's) The paper itself separates "feature reuse" from "low-level statistics" and finds the latter also carries benefit (p.4), so "the fine-tune stays near the base" (CKA, l2, basin) does not isolate reuse of a specific mechanism; closeness in weights is a proxy for reuse, not a definition.

### Kumar, Raghunathan, Jones, Ma, Liang 2022, "Fine-tuning can distort pretrained features and underperform out-of-distribution" (arXiv:2202.10054). Status: main text read (pp. 1-15; proofs and Appendix B, pp. 16-54, not read)
- **Matches the list?** Yes (list gave "Kumar et al. 2022"; authors are Kumar, Raghunathan, Jones, Ma, Liang; this is v1, ICLR 2022 paper).
- **Definition, in the authors' words:** no definition of reuse as such; the paper defines feature distortion and "good pretrained features". p.7: "We call this feature distortion: the features in some directions are changed but not others." p.5, Definition 3.1 (math symbols below retyped by me in plain text; the extraction garbled them): "The distance between feature extractors B, B' in R^{k x d} (with orthonormal rows) is given by (where the min is over rotation matrices U in R^{k x k}): d(B, B') = min_U ||B - U B'||_2". p.5: "Suppose we have a pretrained feature extractor B0 close to B*, so d(B0, B*) <= eps". p.5: "Intuitively, the existence of B* corresponds to assuming that there exists a shared set of useful features for ID (Pid) and OOD (Pood)." Empirical proxy, p.13: "we took the Euclidean distance of the ResNet-50 features before and after fine-tuning: ||g_B(x) - g_B0(x)||_2".
- **What counts as the thing:** a feature extractor g_B(x) (a linear map B in the theory, a deep network in the experiments); the head is a separate linear layer v.
- **What counts as the original model already having it:** the pretrained extractor B0 is "good" when it is within eps (up to rotation) of the true extractor B* that solves both the training and the shifted distribution. Features are preserved if B stays at B0 (linear probing keeps B = B0, p.4).
- **Kind:** formal definition (for distance of a feature extractor to a target one, and for distortion as direction-dependent change) in a toy linear model; operational measure (feature displacement) in experiments. Not a definition of reuse of a mechanism.
- **What it assumes:** two-layer linear network, regression, overparameterised, well-specified target y = v*^T B* x (p.5), a head that is randomly or zero initialised, vanishing step size (gradient flow), a feature extractor/head split, pretrained features that are already good, and a distribution shift to inputs outside the training span.
- **Is it about the fine-tune or about the task?** About the fine-tune and its initialization: with the same task and same features, fine-tuning from a random head distorts features and fine-tuning from a linear-probed head (LP-FT) does not (Prop. 3.7, p.10; LP-FT moves features about 20x less, p.13). So two fine-tunes with the same behaviour in-distribution get different answers. Yes.
- **Objection:** Authors, p.15: "There are several open questions and extensions such as dealing with non-linear activations, different layerwise learning rates, and the effect of explicit regularization." Also p.4-5 note that the main theorem does not need well-specification but the comparison with linear probing does. (reader's) The measure is displacement of features, and the paper itself shows displacement cuts both ways: it harms OOD accuracy in their setting but "fine-tuning can do better than linear probing OOD if the pretrained features are not very high quality" (p.3). So small displacement is not by itself the criterion for "reused vs new".

### Yosinski, Clune, Bengio, Lipson 2014, "How transferable are features in deep neural networks?" (arXiv:1411.1792). Status: main text read (pp. 1-8; supplementary pp. 10+ only skimmed to p.12)
- **Matches the list?** Yes (NIPS 2014; v1 preprint).
- **Definition, in the authors' words:** p.3: "we define the degree of generality of a set of features learned on task A as the extent to which the features can be used for another task B. It is important to note that this definition depends on the similarity between A and B." p.2: "This process will tend to work if the features are general, meaning suitable to both base and target tasks, instead of specific to the base task." p.1: first-layer features "general", last-layer features "specific": "These are intuitive notions of general and specific for which we will provide more rigorous definitions below."
- **What counts as the thing:** the first n layers' weights, copied and either frozen or fine-tuned (p.3: networks BnB, AnB, with + for fine-tuned). A "feature" is the layer's learned filters/activations.
- **What counts as the original model already having it:** the copied layers from base network A, placed under random upper layers and trained on B, perform as well as a network that learned those layers on B itself ("If A3B performs as well as baseB, there is evidence that the third-layer features are general, at least with respect to B", p.3). A control (selffer BnB) is needed because of "fragile co-adaptation" (p.6).
- **Kind:** operational measure (transfer performance AnB vs BnB, per layer, p.3), stated as a definition of generality; experimental findings otherwise.
- **What it assumes:** layered feed-forward CNN (AlexNet-style, 8 layers); same architecture for base and target; transfer by copying the first n layers and retraining the rest from random initialization; pairs of classification tasks A and B cut from one dataset; the answer depends on how similar A and B are (p.3).
- **Is it about the fine-tune or about the task?** About the layer and the task pair, not about a particular fine-tune: it tests whether features trained for A are suitable for B, by retraining only the upper layers, so a fine-tune that changes the copied layers is measured only in the "+" variant by final accuracy. Two fine-tunes with the same accuracy would get the same answer. No (answer is a property of layer plus task pair).
- **Objection:** Authors, p.7, on their random-weight comparison: "The hyperparameter and architectural choices of our network collectively provide one new datapoint, but it may well be possible to tweak layer sizes and random initialization details to enable much better performance for random weights." And p.6 on the frozen-layer measure: the drop "is from a combination of two separate effects: the drop from lost co-adaptation and the drop from features that are less and less general". (reader's) The measure is on frozen copied layers with random upper layers, so it can say "the layer is usable by B", not "the fine-tune left that layer's mechanism in use": with fine-tuning (AnB+) the paper reports only accuracy (p.6, "light red AnB+ ... generalize better").

### Bansal, Nakkiran, Barak 2021, "Revisiting model stitching to compare neural representations" (arXiv:2106.07682). Status: main text read (pp. 1-9; appendix pp. 10+ not read)
- **Matches the list?** Yes (v1, preprint under review at the time; later NeurIPS 2021).
- **Definition, in the authors' words:** model stitching is defined formally, p.4, Eq. 1: "L_l(r; A) = inf_{s in S} L(A_{>l} o s o r)", "the smallest loss obtained by stitching r into all but the first l layers of A using a stitching layer from S. We define the stitching penalty of the representation r with respect to A (as well as l and the loss L) as L_l(r; A) - L(A). If the penalty is non-negative, then we say that the representation r is at least as good as the first l layers of A." p.7, Definition 1: "Let A, B be two networks of identical architectures. We say A and B are stitching-connected if they can be stitched to each other at all layers, with low penalty." On the meaning, p.7: "these two different parameter settings yield essentially equivalent internal representations– this is what it means to be stitching-connected." They do not use the phrase "feature reuse".
- **What counts as the thing:** a "representation" r: X -> R^d, the output of the first layers of some network B (p.3: "the first (bottom-most) layers of some 'bottom model' B").
- **What counts as the original model already having it:** representation r (from model B) is "compatible" with model A if r can replace A's first l layers through a low-capacity trainable stitching layer, with low loss penalty, on the task's test set (p.4-5). It is compared with a ceiling: "stitching is not learning" check, a random bottom network gives a high penalty (p.4).
- **Kind:** formal definition (stitching penalty, stitching connectivity), operational.
- **What it assumes:** two trained networks of the same (or compatible) architecture; layered networks with a cut point at a layer; a task with a loss L; a stitching family S that is simple (1x1 conv, token-wise linear) and has to be chosen; the downstream top network A is fixed and frozen ("two trained and frozen models", abstract p.1).
- **Is it about the fine-tune or about the task?** About the pair of representations and the task loss, not about how a fine-tune got there. Two fine-tunes of one base model that reach the same behaviour: each stitches into the base's top layers; the answer depends only on their representations and not on the path. The authors say p.6: "unlike measures such as CKA, model stitching depends on the downstream task." So: depends on task and endpoints, not on the update path.
- **Objection:** Authors, p.9: "Model stitching does have its limitations: it requires training a network, making it more expensive in computation than other measures such as CKA. Additionally, stitching representations from two different architectures can be tricky and requires a careful choice of the stitching family." Also p.4: "if the stitching family S is sufficiently powerful, it can learn to transform any representation into any other representation." (reader's) The verdict depends on the chosen stitching family and the frozen top, and says nothing on whether the *top* layers of a fine-tune were reused or replaced; it is asymmetric (bottom layers only, p.9 "we restricted ourselves to stitching the first l layers").
