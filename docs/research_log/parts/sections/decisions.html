A copy of a broad fine-tune keeps its behaviour but fails the adversarial check: what next?

What it is about. Teacher training, retraining the subcomponents to imitate a fine-tuned model, is the commute line's working map and the plan for the larger model. Every copy so far was of a fine-tune that acts at one trigger token, and every copy passed. E37 copied a fine-tune that acts at every position: Pile text lowercased, with no trigger.

What the evidence says. One run, independently checked (reports/reviews/2026-10-26_review_teacher-spread.md). The copy keeps all of the fine-tune's gain on held-out lowercase text (0.998 of it) and its predictions sit close to the fine-tune's. Under the standard adversarial check it reads 2.20 times the original model, above the fail line of 1.61. A second batch order reads 1.88 and a copy trained on the same text with capitals kept reads 1.74. Leaving the update in Δ reads 4.87, but that reading is inflated by construction: the adversary may switch off Δ, which then holds the whole update. The scaled adjoint split keeps 0.80 of the gain and passes at 1.05, but it barely changes predictions on ordinary text, while the copies change them as much as the fine-tune does. Across these states the check rises with that change, so the copy may fail because its teacher changes ordinary text a lot, not because the copy is a poor map; this is untested. The copies were still learning: their training loss fell by 33 to 40% between steps 500 and 1000. Two batch orders land on nearly the same weight change (cosine 0.93).

The options. (a) The filed decision tree's own branch, and the cheapest: the copy is still learning, so train it three times longer (about $2), reading the check at several steps and two adversary seeds, before reading anything into the failure. (b) Judge the copy against leaving the update in Δ, option (b) of the anchor card below: it then passes, at 0.45 of do-nothing, but do-nothing's reading is inflated, so this rule flatters every map. (c) For broad updates, take the adjoint split as the map, or start the copy from it; it matches the fine-tune's predictions less closely. (d) If (a) leaves the failure standing, the tree's next step: test whether the frozen Γ (E15's refit, about $9) or the subcomponents' span limits the copy.

Why it matters. The larger model's post-trained update is broad, like this one. If teacher training is faithful only on trigger-like updates, the larger-model run needs a different map or a different pass rule.

Should the adversarial check search from several random starts?

What it is about. The adversarial check searches for the masks on the subcomponents and Δ that most change the model's predictions on ordinary Pile text. It starts from one random setting of the masks and takes 20 small steps. The reading is the damage it finds over the unchanged model's, so 1 means no easier to break. A search that misses worse masks reads too low, so every reading is a lower bound.

What the evidence says. E34 Amendment 5 read one fine-tune left in Δ on the same 128 rows from two random starts: 9.2 from one, 1.7 from the other. The rows, the arithmetic precision and eight times more steps changed little. The unchanged model read within 2.4% from either start. With two draws, which reading is typical is unknown. Amendment 4 had read six fine-tunes from the same two starts, on different rows: the other start read higher on five and lower on none. One of them, which the check passed at 1.12, read 2.09 there.

The options. (a) First measure the spread over starts: eight starts each on the unchanged model, this fine-tune, an E34 copy, the E37 copy and the adjoint split, about $2 on an H100. (b) Make the check the worst of K starts, at K times the cost per reading, and re-read past verdicts. (c) Keep one start and report every reading as a lower bound.

Why it matters. Every one-start "pass" and "inconclusive" since round 2 could be lenient on updates left in Δ, and the larger-model run will use this check. More starts can only raise a state's reading: E37's copy already fails from one start.

Restate the fixed-shift hypothesis without cues?

What it is about. The hypothesis says one fixed change to Γ's importance values per cue word carries the switching after a fine-tune. A cue is the trigger token of our toy tasks, and real post-training has none. Tonight's runs tested a form that names no token: each input or position gets the change of the training inputs the original model treats most alike.

What the evidence says. On the toy tasks it does as well as one change per cue added the same way, and the most similar inputs share the cue 62 to 71% of the time, against a predicted 90% (E35). On a fine-tune of lowercased text, with no trigger, it recovers 0.76 to 0.89 of the switching once fitted on 262,144 positions, better than any other rule (E36). The original model's attention inputs, which Γ reads, do as well; a summary of the last four tokens recovers about half. A bag of the whole preceding text added nothing to those four tokens, but carried no usable signal on its own, so whether longer context closes the gap is still untested. Switching's share of the gain is not evidence on its own: an update that learned nothing showed a similar share.

The options. (a) Restate now, tentatively: "positions the original model treats alike get alike changes", in place of "per cue", noting it holds on the toy tasks and, with enough fitted positions, for three-quarters or more of the switching on one synthetic style of running text; the name "fixed shift" would then need replacing. (b) Keep "per cue" until a fine-tune of a real behaviour that learns confirms the new form; the code-text fine-tune did not learn, and the larger model's post-trained sibling is the natural test. (c) Restate, but count lowercasing as too synthetic, so the claim rests on the toy tasks for now.

Why it matters. A form without cues is what lets the re-gating line say anything about real post-training, which is what the user asked of it.

Should the adversarial check be judged against a fixed line, or against leaving the decomposition alone?

What it is about. The success criterion (reading A) asks the new decomposition to pass VPD's checks on the updated model about as well as the old one does on the original. For the adversarial check we read a ratio: the adversary's damage on the new decomposition over its damage on the original. A ratio under 1.5 passes, with a margin for seed-to-seed noise.

The problem. Do-nothing keeps every subcomponent and puts the whole update into Δ, so it changes nothing that could make the subcomponents fragile. Yet it fails the 1.5 line on large Adam fine-tunes at step 10 (2.18 on emoticon), because the adversary can remove Δ, which now holds the update. So the line mixes two things: how much of the update sits outside the subcomponents, and whether the subcomponents are fragile.

The options. (a) Keep the fixed line, and report do-nothing beside each verdict, as now. (b) Count a state as passing when it is no worse than the same update's do-nothing. (c) Anchor on another baseline, such as a decomposition retrained on the updated model, which costs a full VPD run. Since E21–E22 the choice affects fewer verdicts. Small and later updates' do-nothing passes the line, so only large early updates are read differently.

Why it matters. Under (b), a state that fails the line by no more than its do-nothing does would pass. A state worse than its do-nothing still fails, like the least-squares split of the large fine-tune (5.4 against 2.18). That changes what counts as a map that commutes.
