Our copy of a new behaviour passes the agreed test, yet the parts the decomposition marks as important at the cue cannot reproduce it: should the test change?

What we are deciding. Whether passing the decomposition's own checks on ordinary text is enough when a new behaviour lives in parts the decomposition treats as unimportant at the moment the behaviour is used.

What the evidence says. Teacher training copied fine-tunes that teach the 67M model a word cipher it lacked; the copy keeps the cipher, passes the checks on ordinary text and leaves Δ alone (E42). But at the cue, the model rebuilt from only the parts marked important there puts 0.007 on the right word, where the copy puts 0.96, and ordinary text almost never holds the cue. No method that keeps Γ fixed has avoided this so far. The agreed test (reading A, 2026-10-19, left open to change) passes E42; GOALS.md's mechanism section, marked as not deciding, would fail it.

The options. (a) Keep the agreed test, and report where a new behaviour lives as a finding: E42 passes. (b) Also require the important-parts-only model to keep the behaviour on the task's own text: every fixed-Γ method so far fails, so the next step would be letting Γ change, or a copy trained to meet that requirement. (c) Try such a copy first: tried and failed, as every masked version broke its own nothing-to-copy control (E42 Amendments 1 and 2, four of four at least six times past the fail line), so no masked copy was ever run. (d) Keep the agreed test for the verdict, and print the important-parts reading beside every pass.

Why it matters. It decides whether keeping Γ fixed can work when post-training teaches something new, the project's fork between new capabilities and brittle decompositions.

On the rare-target task, teacher training's closer copy missed both bars narrowly: accept "not placed", or check that copy once more?

What it is about. GOALS.md asks whether post-training strengthens what the decomposition already has or creates something new. The rare-target task is the clearest test of creating: after " in", the fine-tuned model must predict " Fig", to which the unchanged model gives 0.003. Training on the task's labels never placed it cleanly. Teacher training places a task if its copy of the fine-tune, with Γ and Δ frozen, keeps the behaviour (the probability of " Fig" on held-out windows within 0.05 of the fine-tune's) and passes the adversarial check (1.40 or less passes, 1.61 or more fails).

What the evidence says. The fine-tune reached only 0.86 on " Fig", where the other tasks reach 0.9, and left in Δ it reads 11.3 on the check. Teacher training's copy trained on the task's own text reached 0.81 and read 1.45: it missed keeping by 0.006 and the pass line by 4%, so it is inconclusive, not unfaithful. It read 0.87 at step 100 and slid to 0.81 by step 1,000; the adversary was not run at step 100. Its copy trained on plain text alone reached 0.74 and read 2.48, a fail (E34 Amendment 6).

The options. (a) Stop: record that no Γ-frozen map tried has placed the rare target, with the narrow miss stated; free, but the "creates" reading then rests on one copy at one batch order. (b) Rerun the task-text copy to step 100 and run the adversary there, about $1. (c) Rerun it at a second batch order to step 1,000 with the adversary, which tells whether the miss is teacher training's or the batch order's, about $1. (b) and (c) in one job: about $1.5. (d) First make a stronger fine-tune that reaches 0.9 and copy that: a new design and review, a few dollars, and it asks whether a weak fine-tune is the reason.

Recommendation. (b) and (c) together, about $1.5: a pass at either place means teacher training can place the rare target, and two more misses make "not placed" a reading, not a coin toss. If the rare target will be taken up on the larger model, or with Γ allowed to change, anyway, (a) suffices; if a copy of a 0.86 fine-tune is judged uninformative whatever it reads, (d) comes first.

Why it matters. A rare target that no Γ-frozen copy can place is evidence that a decomposition needs new subcomponents or a changed Γ for behaviour the model did not have; a narrow miss at one batch order cannot carry that.

Should the adversarial check be judged against a fixed line, or against leaving the decomposition alone?

What it is about. The success criterion (reading A) asks the new decomposition to pass VPD's checks on the updated model about as well as the old one does on the original. For the adversarial check we read the ratio of the adversary's damage on the new decomposition to its damage on the original; under 1.5 passes.

The problem. Do-nothing keeps every subcomponent and puts the whole update into Δ, so it adds nothing fragile to the subcomponents. Yet it fails the 1.5 line on large Adam fine-tunes at step 10 (2.18 on emoticon), because the adversary can remove Δ, which now holds the update. So the line mixes how much of the update sits outside the subcomponents with whether the subcomponents are fragile.

The options. (a) Keep the fixed line, and report do-nothing beside each verdict, as now. (b) Count a state as passing when it is no worse than the same update's do-nothing. (c) Anchor on a decomposition retrained on the updated model, which costs a full VPD run. Only large early updates are read differently.

Why it matters. Under (b), a state that fails the line by no more than its do-nothing would pass; a state worse than its do-nothing still fails, like the least-squares split of the large fine-tune (5.4 against 2.18).

What would it mean to understand re-gating? Four readings, one to pick

What it is about. Re-gating is the difference in Γ's importance values before and after a fine-tune, with the subcomponents held at their original values; it holds most of a narrow fine-tune (E5). The question now is what the difference means for interpreting the update, and four readings lead to different runs.

What we know.

  • Real and specific. A fine-tune, a one-matrix LoRA and component training switch on largely the same emoticon set; a fine-tune to another token overlaps it by 0.02 to 0.05, and a random update switches on nothing (E6, E18).
  • Partly the model's own machinery, and only half the story. The set overlaps the pieces the original model uses before the same token about ten times more than chance (E18, E29); the set carries under half of the masked model's half-way gain (E4, E6).
  • Putting those pieces back undoes part of the fine-tune. Restoring the set's original trigger outputs takes back about a fifth of the gain on two of three tasks, but pieces ranked by pull on the target take back at least as much at the same count; Γ finds a set rich in them without the target (E38).

Uncertainties. Whether the set carries anything beyond first order; what moved at the trigger to switch them on; whether a Γ fitted to the new model agrees (E15); one 67M model.

Readings. Three ran as E38; a Γ fitted to the new model is parked on E15. Recommendation. Pick the reading; the 67M restore line, which had to be settled first, is closed (user, 2026-10-28). Why it matters. It sets what a fine-tune's write-up says: a piece census, a routing trace, or both.
