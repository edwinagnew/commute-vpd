What would it mean to understand re-gating? Four readings, one to pick

What it is about. Re-gating is the difference in Γ's importance values before and after a fine-tune, with the subcomponents held at their original values; it holds most of a narrow fine-tune (E5). The question now is what the difference means for interpreting the update, and four readings lead to different runs.

What we know.

  • Real and specific. A fine-tune, a one-matrix LoRA and component training switch on largely the same emoticon set; a fine-tune to another token overlaps it by 0.02 to 0.05, and a random update switches on nothing (E6, E18).
  • Partly the model's own machinery, and only half the story. The set overlaps the pieces the original model uses before the same token about ten times more than chance (E18, E29); the set carries under half of the masked model's half-way gain (E4, E6).
  • Putting those pieces back undoes part of the fine-tune. Restoring the set's original trigger outputs takes back about a fifth of the gain on two of three tasks, but pieces ranked by pull on the target take back at least as much at the same count; Γ finds a set rich in them without the target (E38).

Uncertainties. Whether the set carries anything beyond first order; what moved at the trigger to switch them on; whether a Γ fitted to the new model agrees (E15); one 67M model.

Readings. Three ran as E38; a Γ fitted to the new model is parked on E15. Recommendation. Settle that pending card first, then pick the reading. Why it matters. It sets what a fine-tune's write-up says: a piece census, a routing trace, or both.

A ranking by pull on the target takes back at least what the switched-on set adds: close the 67M restore line, take the upstream step, or move the question to Qwen?

What it is about. After a narrow fine-tune, putting back the original trigger outputs of the set Γ switched on takes back about a fifth of the gain, far more than random sets. Does that set carry a part of the gain that subcomponents ranked by their first-order pull on the target, at the same count, miss?

What the evidence says. No restore has shown that it does. A ranking by pull at its count matches it on ` which` and beats it on emoticon; and the two restored together take back close to the sum of the two apart, as two adjacent ranked sets also do. The top set by pull at twice the count, free to take the switched-on set's members, takes back at least what both take back together: clearly more on emoticon, 0.62 and 0.70 of the gain against 0.49 and 0.54, mostly by dropping the switched-on members that push against the target, and the same within noise on ` which`. So the set's share is what its target-pointing members carry; found without the target, it holds about ten times the chance share of the most target-pointing subcomponents (E38, checked). Open: on ` which` the swap gained a third or less of what first order predicted, possibly an effect beyond first order in the members it drops; the parts were not restored apart.

The options. (a) Close the 67M restore line and record the reading: Γ is a detector that needs no target and finds a set rich in the subcomponents that carry a narrow fine-tune's gain; no restore has shown that set carries a part a target-aware ranking at the same count misses. (b) The upstream step: find which earlier changes moved the trigger's inputs so that the set switched on. (c) Carry the question to the Qwen line. Recommended: (a). The tree does not choose; the evidence supports (a)'s reading, and nothing in the restore line argues for (b) or (c), each of which would be chosen for its own question. One $0 restore of the dropped members apart on ` which` would test the open point before closing, if wanted.

Why it matters. It fixes what the re-gating line claims about Γ on the 67M model: that Γ finds, without the target, many of the subcomponents that carry the change, not a share that a target-aware ranking misses.

Should the adversarial check be judged against a fixed line, or against leaving the decomposition alone?

What it is about. The success criterion (reading A) asks the new decomposition to pass VPD's checks on the updated model about as well as the old one does on the original. For the adversarial check we read a ratio: the adversary's damage on the new decomposition over its damage on the original. A ratio under 1.5 passes, with a margin for seed-to-seed noise.

The problem. Do-nothing keeps every subcomponent and puts the whole update into Δ, so it changes nothing that could make the subcomponents fragile. Yet it fails the 1.5 line on large Adam fine-tunes at step 10 (2.18 on emoticon), because the adversary can remove Δ, which now holds the update. So the line mixes two things: how much of the update sits outside the subcomponents, and whether the subcomponents are fragile.

The options. (a) Keep the fixed line, and report do-nothing beside each verdict, as now. (b) Count a state as passing when it is no worse than the same update's do-nothing. (c) Anchor on another baseline, such as a decomposition retrained on the updated model, which costs a full VPD run. Since E21–E22 the choice affects fewer verdicts. Small and later updates' do-nothing passes the line, so only large early updates are read differently.

Why it matters. Under (b), a state that fails the line by no more than its do-nothing does would pass. A state worse than its do-nothing still fails, like the least-squares split of the large fine-tune (5.4 against 2.18). That changes what counts as a map that commutes.
