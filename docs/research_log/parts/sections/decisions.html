The re-gating share cannot tell a fine-tune that learned a rule from one that memorised shuffled labels. Can steering or single neurons? Two choices stand in the way of finding out.

Why this matters. The paper's second part asks whether the re-gating share measures anything simpler methods miss. The share is the part of a fine-tune's gain in the answer's log-odds that appears when we keep the decomposition's original pieces and change only which ones switch on. Our hardest test uses two fine-tunes on the same text. One learns a real rule: after a word, say label A or B according to a property of that word. Its twin gets the same words with labels shuffled, so it can only memorise. The share reads the two alike. If steering or single neurons can tell them apart, the share misses something they see; if neither can, no method we have does.

First choice: where the per-word steering vectors come from. A steering vector is the average change a fine-tune makes to the model's activations. You asked for one per word, built from some examples and tested on others. But the share was measured on 256 examples, and they repeat a word too rarely: only 15 of 128 test examples on the case task, and 34 of 128 on the repeat task, have a second example of their word.

  • Option a: build the vectors from the examples the fine-tune trained on, about 16 per word, and test on the share's 256 examples. Every example is covered. Limit: the fine-tune has seen those training examples, so its vectors may look a little better than on new text.
  • Option b: use all 1,024 held-out examples, half to build and half to test, and re-measure the share on the test half. Limit: on the case task only 205 of the 512 test examples have a vector.
  • Option c: one vector per label instead of per word. Every example is covered. Limit: steering is handed the correct answer on both fine-tunes, so the result says little.

Second choice: only two trained copies of each fine-tune exist. How much copies (the same fine-tune from different seeds) disagree is the noise. Our rule is that the smallest difference that would change our minds, a tenth of the gain, must be at least twice the noise. With two copies it is 1.8 times; three would clear it, but a third copy means new fine-tunes, which your instructions currently rule out.

  • Option d: run with two copies and report what is seen, as a description and not a test. Free, minutes. A small difference cannot be told from noise, and you would have to waive the rule, which the run-number tool otherwise enforces.
  • Option e: train a third copy of each, four more fine-tunes, free, under half an hour (an estimate). The result counts as a test. Limit: you drop the no-new-fine-tunes instruction and add this as new work.
  • Option f: do not run this comparison. Whether any activation-level method sees real against shuffled stays open.

What happens next. After your choice the code is written and run the same day, and you get one figure and one paragraph, with no verdict on reuse.

Recommendation. Options a and d: every example is covered, and your note on this work already said to describe the two-copy result rather than test it, though the rule still needs waiving.
