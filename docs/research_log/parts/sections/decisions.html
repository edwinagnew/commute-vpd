Re-gating line · open since 2026-10-22 (E28)

Should the working hypothesis cover every task, or only tasks the model could already partly do?

What it is about. The re-gating line's working hypothesis, the fixed-shift hypothesis, says that after a narrow fine-tune, the part of the gain that comes from Γ switching existing subcomponents up or down is carried mostly by one fixed change to Γ's importance values. That change is the same for every input. As adopted, it makes no restriction on the kind of task.

What the evidence says. E28 tested the fixed-shift hypothesis on seven tasks it had never been fitted on. On four word tasks, which the model already partly does before fine-tuning, it held: the fixed change gives back 0.74 to 0.99 of the effect of each input's own values. On three key-value tasks, which the model cannot do before fine-tuning, it failed. On the two ciphers that is expected, because there switching carries only a small part of the gain (E12). The copy task is the real exception. Switching carries most of its gain (0.80), yet the fixed change gives back only 0.07.

The options. (a) Restrict the fixed-shift hypothesis to tasks the model already does. It then stands on seven tasks, and the line moves on to a larger model. (b) Keep the fixed-shift hypothesis general and treat the copy task as a counterexample to explain first. A cheap test bears on both: compute the fixed change separately for each key of the copy task. If that gives back 0.7 or more, the failure came from averaging over 32 keys, and the fixed-shift hypothesis needs a smaller amendment than (a).

Why it matters. It decides what the re-gating line runs next, and how the claim is worded in every write-up.

Commute line · open since 2026-10-22 (E31)

The adjoint split kept a small SGD fine-tune's behaviour on one task but not on two others, trained at three times the rate. Should the next run tell the rate from the task?

What it is about. The adjoint split rewrites a finished fine-tune as changes to the existing subcomponents, leaving Δ unchanged. It is the one map so far that stays faithful at every step, but it can lose the trained behaviour. One case keeps it: a fine-tune by plain SGD (no momentum, no per-weight rescaling as in Adam) at learning rate 0.01, on the emoticon task. If that held on other tasks, the adjoint split of small SGD fine-tunes would be the first map that meets the weak version on several tasks.

What the evidence says. On emoticon, after 1,000 fine-tune steps, the split SGD model gives the target token a probability of 0.60 and 0.57 on two seeds. An Adam fine-tune of similar size keeps only 0.24 and 0.21. Every one of these split models stays faithful. On two word tasks (" which" then " was", and " it" then " is") the split SGD model keeps only 0.07 and 0.26, about what a large Adam fine-tune keeps (0.05 and 0.23). But there the SGD rate was 0.03, not 0.01. The run picked each task's rate from how well it learned after 10 steps, and at 0.01 the word tasks reached a target probability of 0.84, below the rule's bar. At 0.03 the SGD update grew nearly as large as the large Adam one by step 1,000. Its most-changed matrix moved by about 3% of that matrix's own size, against 3.5% for large Adam. Emoticon's SGD update moved by 1.2%. So the run cannot tell whether the task or the larger rate, and so the larger update, removed the effect.

The options. (a) Rerun SGD at 0.01 on the two word tasks for 1,000 steps and split it at steps 10, 100 and 1,000: one cluster test job, under an hour, no cost. Its update should end near emoticon's size, so it tests rate and size together. If the split model keeps at least half the target probability, the effect follows small updates and not the emoticon task. If it loses it while the fine-tune itself has learned the task, the effect belongs to emoticon. If the fine-tune at 0.01 has not learned the task by step 1,000, the run says nothing either way, and (b) is the fallback. (b) Match by size rather than rate: pick each word task's SGD rate so that its update at step 1,000 is as large as emoticon's SGD update, then compare. This is cleaner when the rate alone gives a different size, and costs a short rate search first. (c) Stop here: record the emoticon result as a one-task finding and give the commute line's next run to the shift map, applying the raised and lowered halves of Γ's fixed change separately, then the change only where the fine-tuned model's own values move.

Why it matters. It decides the commute line's next run. Under (a) or (b), a kept behaviour makes the adjoint split of small SGD fine-tunes a candidate map on several tasks; a lost one closes that candidate. Under (c) the candidate stays open on one task.

Commute line · open since 2026-10-21

Should the adversarial check be judged against a fixed line, or against leaving the decomposition alone?

What it is about. The success criterion (reading A) asks the new decomposition to pass VPD's checks on the updated model about as well as the old one does on the original. For the adversarial check we read a ratio: the adversary's damage on the new decomposition over its damage on the original. A ratio under 1.5 passes, with a margin for seed-to-seed noise.

The problem. Do-nothing keeps every subcomponent and puts the whole update into Δ, so it changes nothing that could make the subcomponents fragile. Yet it fails the 1.5 line on large Adam fine-tunes at step 10 (2.18 on emoticon), because the adversary can remove Δ, which now holds the update. So the line mixes two things: how much of the update sits outside the subcomponents, and whether the subcomponents are fragile.

The options. (a) Keep the fixed line, and report do-nothing beside each verdict, as now. (b) Count a state as passing when it is no worse than the same update's do-nothing. (c) Anchor on another baseline, such as a decomposition retrained on the updated model, which costs a full VPD run. Since E21–E22 the choice affects fewer verdicts. Small and later updates' do-nothing passes the line, so only large early updates are read differently.

Why it matters. Under (b), a state that fails the line by no more than its do-nothing does would pass. A state worse than its do-nothing still fails, like the least-squares split of the large fine-tune (5.4 against 2.18). That changes what counts as a map that commutes.
