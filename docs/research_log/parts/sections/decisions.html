Is GSM8K hard enough for the unchanged Qwen3-1.7B, and what happens to answers longer than the decomposition's window?

What it is about. Before fine-tuning Qwen3-1.7B on GSM8K we measured the unchanged model on all 1,319 test problems, with thinking off. Two choices follow from it that change the first experiment's plan.

What the evidence says. The model solves 0.756 of problems by the strict grading rule. The filed decision tree says above 0.75 means too little headroom and the task should change, so its letter is triggered, by half a standard error. A quarter of problems are still wrong, which is far from ceiling. Nineteen answers in twenty fit inside the 512 tokens the decomposition was fitted on, but the ones past it are mostly wrong: 0.32 right against 0.78 inside. Two prompts in five give both right and wrong samples, enough for reinforcement learning later.

The options. Headroom: (a) keep GSM8K as it is, the report's reading; (b) switch to one of the scoping report's harder variants before any fine-tune. Over-long answers in the held-out set: (c) drop them, which keeps every scored answer complete but removes the hardest problems; (d) cap them at 512 tokens, which keeps the problem mix but scores 5% of answers without their final number. Whichever is chosen, the other is reported beside it.

Why it matters. Dropping the over-long answers tilts the held-out set toward problems the model already solves, so a fine-tune that mostly helps hard problems would look weaker than it is. A format fix (the model often writes the number as \boxed{N}) goes ahead either way and is not part of this choice.

Qwen3-1.7B line · new 2026-10-27

Nothing runs until this is decided; the first Qwen experiment's plan waits on it.

Does the paper's stress test break the unchanged Qwen decomposition, or did we run the test too small?

What it is about. The VPD paper checks a decomposition with an adversary: it searches for the worst way to turn subcomponents partly on or off, within what their importances allow, and measures how far the model's predictions move. A faithful decomposition barely moves. On the 67M testbed this was the main pass or fail test.

What the evidence says. On four rows of ordinary web text the unchanged Qwen decomposition moves the predictions by about 20 nats under the adversary, in the authors' own code and in our port alike (three random starts each, readings 7 to 28). Simply setting each subcomponent to its importance moves them by 0.15 nats. Twenty nats means the predictions are wrecked. The authors run the test on 128 rows at once, and the adversary picks one setting per subcomponent shared by every row and position, so on four rows it can tailor itself far more and should read much higher. That explanation is untested. The readings are also noisy: one start cannot tell apart two states less than three times apart.

The options. (a) Run the test at the authors' size now, 128 rows of 512 tokens, as one free cluster job of about half an hour per start, before the first experiment is planned. (b) Run it as the first experiment's first stage, beside the check of the decomposition on GSM8K text. (c) Drop the adversary for Qwen and judge faithfulness by the ordinary masked comparison alone.

Why it matters. If the test also reads high at 128 rows, it cannot be the pass or fail test on Qwen that it was on the testbed, and the first experiment needs another way to say a decomposition still describes the model after an update.

Re-gating line · new 2026-10-27

Due to run: E38 Amendment 3, the restore test (one cluster job, at most 15 minutes), chosen by the user on 2026-10-27; this card is re-read after it.

What would it mean to understand re-gating? Four readings, one to pick

What it is about. Re-gating is the difference in Γ's importance values before and after a fine-tune, measured with the subcomponents held at their original values. Changes to the subcomponents themselves carry under a tenth of the gain (E5), so every narrow fine-tune we have run shows up mostly in re-gating. The fixed-shift hypothesis, which described the shape of that difference on toy tasks, was de-prioritised on 2026-10-27. The question now is what the difference means for interpreting the update; four readings lead to different runs.

What we know.

  • The difference is real and specific. A fine-tune, a one-matrix LoRA and component training switch on largely the same emoticon set (overlaps 0.82 and 0.64, E6), a fine-tune to a different token at the same trigger overlaps it by only 0.02 to 0.05 (E6, E18), and a random update switches on nothing.
  • It is partly the model's own machinery, and only half the story. The set overlaps the pieces the original model uses before the same token about ten times more than chance (E18, E29), and with the pieces untouched it takes the masked model about half-way to the target, of which the set carries under half (E4, E6).
  • The fine-tuned model leans on those pieces, but they also work without the fine-tune. Without the set it loses a tenth to a quarter more of its gain than the original does, more than for random sets; a set switched on by a different fine-tune shows some of the same (E38).
  • The share on its own says little. A fine-tune that learned nothing read the same share as one that learned (E36): Γ is the only part that depends on context.

The biggest uncertainties. How much of the fine-tune acts through those pieces (the restore test, due to run); what moved in the residual stream at the trigger to make Γ switch them on, which nobody has measured; whether a Γ fitted to the new model would say the same (E15); and that everything rests on one 67M model and trigger tasks.

The readings. Three ran as E38; the fourth, a Γ fitted to the new model, is parked on E15.

Recommendation. Read the restore test's result first, then pick the reading. Plan: reports/plans/2026-10-27_plan_regating-recruitment.md.

Why it matters. This sets what the write-up of a fine-tune will say: a piece census, a routing trace from the update to the pieces, or both.

Commute line · open since 2026-10-21

Nothing waits on this; every verdict since E21 reports both readings. A random-move control for it runs inside E39 (section {{n:next}}, commute line).

Should the adversarial check be judged against a fixed line, or against leaving the decomposition alone?

What it is about. The success criterion (reading A) asks the new decomposition to pass VPD's checks on the updated model about as well as the old one does on the original. For the adversarial check we read a ratio: the adversary's damage on the new decomposition over its damage on the original. A ratio under 1.5 passes, with a margin for seed-to-seed noise.

The problem. Do-nothing keeps every subcomponent and puts the whole update into Δ, so it changes nothing that could make the subcomponents fragile. Yet it fails the 1.5 line on large Adam fine-tunes at step 10 (2.18 on emoticon), because the adversary can remove Δ, which now holds the update. So the line mixes two things: how much of the update sits outside the subcomponents, and whether the subcomponents are fragile.

The options. (a) Keep the fixed line, and report do-nothing beside each verdict, as now. (b) Count a state as passing when it is no worse than the same update's do-nothing. (c) Anchor on another baseline, such as a decomposition retrained on the updated model, which costs a full VPD run. Since E21–E22 the choice affects fewer verdicts. Small and later updates' do-nothing passes the line, so only large early updates are read differently.

Why it matters. Under (b), a state that fails the line by no more than its do-nothing does would pass. A state worse than its do-nothing still fails, like the least-squares split of the large fine-tune (5.4 against 2.18). That changes what counts as a map that commutes.
