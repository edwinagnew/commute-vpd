What would it mean to understand re-gating? Four readings, one to pick

What it is about. Re-gating is the difference in Γ's importance values before and after a fine-tune, with the subcomponents held at their original values; it holds most of a narrow fine-tune (E5). The question now is what the difference means for interpreting the update, and four readings lead to different runs.

What we know.

  • Real and specific. A fine-tune, a one-matrix LoRA and component training switch on largely the same emoticon set (E6); a fine-tune to another token overlaps it by 0.02 to 0.05 (E6, E18); a random update switches on nothing.
  • Partly the model's own machinery, and only half the story. The set overlaps the pieces the original model uses before the same token about ten times more than chance (E18, E29); the set carries under half of the masked model's half-way gain (E4, E6).
  • Putting those pieces back undoes part of the fine-tune. Restoring the set's original trigger outputs takes back about a fifth of the gain on two of three tasks; the pieces whose outputs moved most do not, but as many pieces picked for pointing toward the target do as well or better (E38).

Uncertainties. Whether the set and the target-pointing pieces carry the same part of the gain (pending card); what moved at the trigger to switch them on; whether a Γ fitted to the new model agrees (E15); one 67M model.

Readings. Three ran as E38; a Γ fitted to the new model is parked on E15. Recommendation. Settle that pending card first, then pick the reading. Why it matters. It sets what a fine-tune's write-up says: a piece census, a routing trace, or both.

Subcomponents picked for pointing toward the target take back as much as the switched-on set: close the 67M restore line, test whether the two sets carry the same part of the gain, or move on?

What it is about. After a narrow fine-tune, putting the trigger outputs of the set Γ switched on back to their original values takes back about a fifth of the gain. We asked whether any subcomponents whose changes point toward the target would do as well. We ranked them by each one's first-order pull on the target, which needs the target and no Γ, and took the switched-on set's count in each matrix.

What the evidence says. That set takes back more than the switched-on set on emoticon, 0.35 and 0.40 of the gain against 0.24 and 0.22, and about the same on ` which`, a tie inside the noise floor. So the switched-on set is never ahead. On emoticon the gap comes mostly from switched-on subcomponents that push against the target. Still, the switched-on set, found without the target, holds about ten times the chance share of the most target-pointing subcomponents, and a ranking by size of change does not find its share (E38, checked). Not tested: whether the two sets carry the same part of the gain.

The options. (a) Close the 67M restore line and record the reading: Γ's set carries no more than target-pointing subcomponents do, and Γ finds many of them without the target. (b) The upstream step: find which earlier changes moved the trigger's inputs so that the set switched on. (c) Carry the question to the Qwen line. (d) Running: first restore both sets together, one cluster debug job on the saved fine-tunes, $0: about 0.4 on ` which` means the switched-on set holds a part the target ranking misses, about 0.2 means they are interchangeable. Recommended: (d), then (a); (d) is free and decides which reading (a) records.

Why it matters. It decides what the re-gating line claims about Γ on the 67M model: that it names the subcomponents carrying the change, or only some of them among equals.

Teacher training on Qwen3-1.7B does not fit one H100: a larger card, lower precision, fewer subcomponents, or half the layers at a time?

What it is about. Teacher training moves every alive subcomponent, and each needs trainable offsets plus the optimiser's state.

What the evidence says. E40's first check finds 494,598 of 688,128 subcomponents alive on maths and web text (importance above 0.01 anywhere). Their offsets and Adam's state need about 73 GB, which does not fit an 80 GB H100 beside the model.

The options. (a) An H200 or B200 pod, same method; recommended if the pod's region (US-MO-1) stocks one. (b) Adam's moments and the alive copies in bf16 on an H100, about 51 GB. (c) A stricter alive set. (d) Half the layers at a time.

Why it matters. Teacher training is the map chosen for the larger model; until this is settled, E40's update at steps 1, 10 and 100 is scored by the do-nothing state only.

Why does a rank-8 LoRA's trained decomposition pass the adversarial check when SGD's, nearly as concentrated, fails?

What it is about. E39 asked why a decomposition trained to copy a broad fine-tune fails the check, and, as you asked, whether SGD or LoRA updates, which are less diffuse, do better.

What the evidence says. The LoRA's copy passes at 1.27 (1.40 or less passes). Should the adversarial check be judged against a fixed line, or against leaving the decomposition alone?

What it is about. The success criterion (reading A) asks the new decomposition to pass VPD's checks on the updated model about as well as the old one does on the original. For the adversarial check we read a ratio: the adversary's damage on the new decomposition over its damage on the original. A ratio under 1.5 passes, with a margin for seed-to-seed noise.

The problem. Do-nothing keeps every subcomponent and puts the whole update into Δ, so it changes nothing that could make the subcomponents fragile. Yet it fails the 1.5 line on large Adam fine-tunes at step 10 (2.18 on emoticon), because the adversary can remove Δ, which now holds the update. So the line mixes two things: how much of the update sits outside the subcomponents, and whether the subcomponents are fragile.

The options. (a) Keep the fixed line, and report do-nothing beside each verdict, as now. (b) Count a state as passing when it is no worse than the same update's do-nothing. (c) Anchor on another baseline, such as a decomposition retrained on the updated model, which costs a full VPD run. Since E21–E22 the choice affects fewer verdicts. Small and later updates' do-nothing passes the line, so only large early updates are read differently.

Why it matters. Under (b), a state that fails the line by no more than its do-nothing does would pass. A state worse than its do-nothing still fails, like the least-squares split of the large fine-tune (5.4 against 2.18). That changes what counts as a map that commutes.
