What would it mean to understand re-gating? Four readings, one to pick

What it is about. Re-gating is the difference in Γ's importance values before and after a fine-tune, with the subcomponents held at their original values; it holds most of a narrow fine-tune (E5). The question now is what the difference means for interpreting the update, and four readings lead to different runs.

What we know.

  • Real and specific. A fine-tune, a one-matrix LoRA and component training switch on largely the same emoticon set (E6); a fine-tune to another token overlaps it by 0.02 to 0.05 (E6, E18); a random update switches on nothing.
  • Partly the model's own machinery, and only half the story. The set overlaps the pieces the original model uses before the same token about ten times more than chance (E18, E29); the set carries under half of the masked model's half-way gain (E4, E6).
  • Putting those pieces back undoes part of the fine-tune. Restoring the set's trigger outputs to the original model's values takes back about a fifth of the gain on two of three tasks; random sets, another fine-tune's set and other active pieces take back about nothing (E38).

Uncertainties. Whether those pieces are special or just moved most (pending card); what moved at the trigger to switch them on; whether a Γ fitted to the new model agrees (E15); one 67M model.

Readings. Three ran as E38; a Γ fitted to the new model is parked on E15. Recommendation. Settle the matched-change card first, then pick the reading. Why it matters. It sets what a fine-tune's write-up says: a piece census, a routing trace, or both.

Putting the switched-on set back undoes part of the fine-tune, and other active pieces do not: is that because the set changed most?

What it is about. After a narrow fine-tune, Γ switches a set of the original model's subcomponents on at the trigger. Putting that set's trigger outputs back to their original-model values takes back part of the gain. Open: does the set matter because Γ switched it on, or only because its outputs changed most?

What the evidence says. Putting the set back takes back 0.22 to 0.24 of the gain on emoticon, 0.18 to 0.20 on ` which` and 0.04 to 0.05 on ` we`; random sets, another fine-tune's set and already-active sets take back 0.05 or less on the word tasks. By the filed rule ` which` is specific, against the prediction, ` we` is not, and emoticon is unscored because too few pieces are active to match the set; scored, it would read specific. The check found the twenty active draws nearly one set (on emoticon each holds the same 107 top-layer pieces) and none matched on output change. Unexplained, provisional: putting back emoticon's active pieces raises the target, by about 0.2 of the gain.

The options. (a) On the saved fine-tunes, restore the pieces outside the set whose trigger outputs changed most, matched per layer, plus emoticon's top-layer active pool alone: one cluster job, $0, recommended. (b) Accept "specific on two of three tasks, provisional" into Q5. (c) Stop the 67M line, or carry the test to the Qwen line.

Why it matters. It decides whether Γ's set names pieces that carry the change, or only those that moved most.

Why does a rank-8 LoRA's trained decomposition pass the adversarial check when SGD's, nearly as concentrated, fails?

What it is about. E39 asked why a decomposition trained to copy a broad fine-tune fails the check, and, as you asked, whether SGD or LoRA updates, which are less diffuse, do better.

What the evidence says. The LoRA's copy passes at 1.27 (1.40 or less passes). An SGD fine-tune with the same gain and an update nearly as concentrated (stable rank 3.5 against the LoRA's 1.9 and Adam's 28.5) does less damage to plain text, yet its copy fails at 2.07, like Adam's 2.20. So being less diffuse is not enough; the LoRA's pass is one seed at one rate, and what sets it apart is untested.

The options. (a) Leave it open and carry on with the Qwen line. (b) Take it up with the report's cheap tests: SGD held to rank 8 per matrix, a random move matched in output change, a second seed on a second set of rows. No recommendation is made.

Why it matters. Teacher training is the map chosen for the larger model, and post-training updates are often low-rank; if the LoRA's pass is real, that is where it works.

Should the adversarial check be judged against a fixed line, or against leaving the decomposition alone?

What it is about. The success criterion (reading A) asks the new decomposition to pass VPD's checks on the updated model about as well as the old one does on the original. For the adversarial check we read a ratio: the adversary's damage on the new decomposition over its damage on the original. A ratio under 1.5 passes, with a margin for seed-to-seed noise.

The problem. Do-nothing keeps every subcomponent and puts the whole update into Δ, so it changes nothing that could make the subcomponents fragile. Yet it fails the 1.5 line on large Adam fine-tunes at step 10 (2.18 on emoticon), because the adversary can remove Δ, which now holds the update. So the line mixes two things: how much of the update sits outside the subcomponents, and whether the subcomponents are fragile.

The options. (a) Keep the fixed line, and report do-nothing beside each verdict, as now. (b) Count a state as passing when it is no worse than the same update's do-nothing. (c) Anchor on another baseline, such as a decomposition retrained on the updated model, which costs a full VPD run. Since E21–E22 the choice affects fewer verdicts. Small and later updates' do-nothing passes the line, so only large early updates are read differently.

Why it matters. Under (b), a state that fails the line by no more than its do-nothing does would pass. A state worse than its do-nothing still fails, like the least-squares split of the large fine-tune (5.4 against 2.18). That changes what counts as a map that commutes.
