Trained four times longer or from a passing start, the decomposition of a broad fine-tune stays twice as fragile: what next?

What it is about. Teacher training retrains the subcomponents so the decomposition imitates a fine-tuned model, Γ and Δ frozen; it is the commute line's working map for the larger model. On fine-tunes that act at one trigger token it passes the paper's adversarial check. E37's fine-tune changes every position (Pile text lowercased): the behaviour was kept, but the adversary did about twice the damage it does on the original decomposition, above the fail line.

What the evidence says. Four times longer left the check at 2.20 times the original at step 1,000 and 2.23 at step 4,000, while the gap to the fine-tuned model kept shrinking and the training loss fell 44% over the last doubling. The filed plan had no branch for that. Started from the adjoint split, which passes at 1.05, the check read 1.76 after 1,000 steps and 1.84 after 2,000. Both rose as the decomposition came to change ordinary text as much as the fine-tune does, but that cause is untested: a control with a thirtieth of that damage failed too. VPD's masked losses could not be tried: their control, the recipe with nothing to copy, read 3.84, as E15's refit control also left the floor.

The options. (a) Train longer still, the plan's extend branch: to 16,000 steps, about $1.5; a fall under the fail line would put under-training back as the explanation. (b) Test whether the frozen Γ is the limit: E15's refit on the trained decomposition, about $9 per state, after a $1 control with the masked losses off. (c) Test whether the subcomponents' span can express the change on ordinary text at all; needs /design first. (d) Settle the anchor question for broad updates (card decision-pass-anchor): count a state as passing when it is no worse than the same update left in Δ. Every trained decomposition then passes, at 0.36 to 0.46 of its 4.87, but that reference is inflated by construction; a $1 control, moving the subcomponents at random by the masked control's size, would inform it. (e) Take teacher training to the larger model as it is, reporting both references.

Why it matters. Real post-training is a broad update. If teacher training is faithful only on trigger-like updates, the larger model needs a different map, a repaired Γ, or a pass rule that fits broad updates.

Re-gating line · new 2026-10-27

Nothing runs on the re-gating line until this is decided.

The switched-on pieces already push toward the target before any fine-tuning: how do we find out how much of the fine-tune runs through them?

What it is about. After a narrow fine-tune, Γ switches on a set of the original model's subcomponents at the trigger (the recruited set). E38 removed that set from the fine-tuned model's weights, to see whether the fine-tune's new behaviour goes with it. Removal takes away a third to a half of the fine-tune's gain in the target's log-odds on two of three tasks, far more than random sets of the same size.

What the evidence says. Removing the same set from the original model, where it was predicted to do nothing, already takes away a sixth to a quarter of that gain; on we-just it takes more than from the fine-tuned model. These pieces already push toward the target before any fine-tuning, so the test cannot yet say how much of the fine-tune works through them. Removing them also disturbs other positions 6 to 22 times as much as the fine-tune itself on two tasks. An independent check found no code error: the set was chosen by a rise in importance, with no bound on its importance before the fine-tune, and the removal acted at every position.

The options. (a) An amendment, about $0.20 and 15 minutes: rerun the test removing pieces at the trigger position only, remove the random sets and the http fine-tune's set from the original model too, score the difference between the fine-tuned and original removal against the random sets' difference, and save the census counts so each piece's own target ratio can be scored. (b) Accept: re-gating's switched-on pieces are ones that already carry the target in the full model; write that into Q5 and stop the line. (c) Redefine the recruited set as the pieces nearly off before the fine-tune (original importance below 0.1) and rerun. The lead recommends (a).

Why it matters. It decides whether the switched-on pieces tell us about the fine-tune or only about pieces already doing that work, and so whether the re-gating line has anything left to interpret.

What would it mean to understand re-gating? Four readings, one to pick

What it is about. Re-gating is the difference in Γ's importance values before and after a fine-tune. It is measured with the subcomponents held at their original values, to separate it from changes to the subcomponents themselves. Experiments that also changed the subcomponents found those changes carry under a tenth of the gain (E5) and barely move where the behaviour sits (E19). So every narrow fine-tune we have run shows up mostly in re-gating. The fixed-shift hypothesis, which described the shape of that difference on toy tasks, was de-prioritised on 2026-10-27. The question now is what the difference means for interpreting the update. There are four things that could mean, and they lead to different runs.

What we know.

  • The difference is real and specific. Which pieces switch on depends on the target far more than on how the update was made: a fine-tune, a one-matrix LoRA and component training switch on largely the same emoticon set (overlaps 0.82 and 0.64, E6; two seeds of one fine-tune reach 0.93), while a fine-tune to a different token at the same trigger overlaps it by only 0.02 to 0.05 (E6, E18). A random update switches on nothing.
  • It is partly the model's own machinery. The set overlaps the pieces the original model uses before the same token in ordinary text about ten times more than chance, but fewer than half of the set are such pieces (E18, E29).
  • It is only half the story. With the pieces untouched, the new values take the masked model about half-way to the target at the trigger; the switched-on set carries under half of that (E4, E6).
  • The fine-tuned model does use those pieces, but they also work without the fine-tune. Removing the switched-on set from the fine-tuned weights takes away a third to a half of the gain on two of three tasks, far more than random sets; removing it from the original model already takes away a sixth to a quarter, so how much of the fine-tune runs through it is not yet measured (E38).
  • The share on its own says little. A fine-tune that learned nothing read the same re-gating share as one that learned (E36), because Γ is the only part of the decomposition that depends on context.

The biggest uncertainties.

  • How much of the fine-tune acts through those pieces? First tested in the real model (E38), but the pieces already push toward the target in the original model, so the share is not yet separated (card above).
  • What moved to make Γ switch them on? The pieces barely change, so the cause must be a shift in the residual stream at the trigger. Nobody has measured where it moved.
  • Would a Γ fitted to the new model say the same? Unknown; the refit's own control fails (E15).
  • Everything rests on one 67M model, ten-step fine-tunes and trigger tasks.

The readings. The user chose on 2026-10-27 to run, in order, a recruitment test, a routing trace and a piece census; all three ran as E38. The fourth reading, whether a Γ fitted to the new model would say the same, is parked (blocked on E15's refit control).

Recommendation. How to go on after E38 is the Blocking card above. Plan: reports/plans/2026-10-27_plan_regating-recruitment.md.

Why it matters. This sets what the write-up of a fine-tune will say: a piece census, a routing trace from the update to the pieces, or both. It also decides whether the re-gating line continues at all.

Commute line · open since 2026-10-21

Nothing runs until the card above is decided, and its option (d) is this question; every verdict since E21 reports both readings.

Should the adversarial check be judged against a fixed line, or against leaving the decomposition alone?

What it is about. The success criterion (reading A) asks the new decomposition to pass VPD's checks on the updated model about as well as the old one does on the original. For the adversarial check we read a ratio: the adversary's damage on the new decomposition over its damage on the original. A ratio under 1.5 passes, with a margin for seed-to-seed noise.

The problem. Do-nothing keeps every subcomponent and puts the whole update into Δ, so it changes nothing that could make the subcomponents fragile. Yet it fails the 1.5 line on large Adam fine-tunes at step 10 (2.18 on emoticon), because the adversary can remove Δ, which now holds the update. So the line mixes two things: how much of the update sits outside the subcomponents, and whether the subcomponents are fragile.

The options. (a) Keep the fixed line, and report do-nothing beside each verdict, as now. (b) Count a state as passing when it is no worse than the same update's do-nothing. (c) Anchor on another baseline, such as a decomposition retrained on the updated model, which costs a full VPD run. Since E21–E22 the choice affects fewer verdicts. Small and later updates' do-nothing passes the line, so only large early updates are read differently.

Why it matters. Under (b), a state that fails the line by no more than its do-nothing does would pass. A state worse than its do-nothing still fails, like the least-squares split of the large fine-tune (5.4 against 2.18). That changes what counts as a map that commutes.
