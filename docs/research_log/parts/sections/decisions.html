Under Jain et al.'s definition, every causal check so far says the case-label fine-tune does not use the original model's case reading. How should we test that next?

The question. Under the adopted definition (Jain et al. 2024), a fine-tune reuses a capability if a linear readout trained on Pile text finds it in the original model, the fine-tune's answer is a simple function of that readout that works on unseen inputs, and removing or patching the readout's direction breaks the fine-tuned behaviour. Our case-label task passes the first two and fails the causal check. Is the check too strict for this small model, or does the fine-tune really not use the readout? No option changes the definition.

The task. After a trigger token, output one of two label words depending on whether the previous word is capitalised. The twin uses the same labels assigned to words at random.

The evidence.

  • The original model holds case: a linear readout at block 1 reads it at 0.994, against 0.524 for a control readout (card, report).
  • The fine-tune is right on unseen words (0.998) and acts like a threshold on that readout (0.99). The twin is at chance on both.
  • Removing the readout direction takes 8-9% of the gain, and removing two directions at every position 19-21%, against 50% needed. Full erasure could not be read: a random removal of the same size already took 19-26%, and erasure doubled the original model's next-token loss.
  • Patching the case value flips the original model's own later case reading 71% of the time, but the fine-tuned answer only 0.2-1.4% of the time (card, report).
  • So the case-label fine-tune reads "not through the capability". The copy-versus-lookup pair has never been read under this definition, because no Pile readout of copying exists. So the check has never said "reuses" on the small model, and we cannot tell whether the check or the task is at fault (line summary, figure of every causal check).

Options.

  • (A) Give the copy fine-tune a Pile readout and run the same checks. Runs: train a linear readout of "the token that followed this token earlier in the text" on Pile text in the original model, then run the same four checks (unseen inputs, simple function, removal, patching) on the saved copy and lookup fine-tunes. Tells you whether the check can ever say "reuses" on a fine-tune we have other evidence reuses a part: removing one attention head takes about half of copy's gain and none of lookup's (card). Next: if copy also reads "not through", the check is too strict for this model; if it reads "reuses", the case result stands as a finding about that task. Cost: $0, a day of code and minutes of cluster time. Limit: if the copying readout fails its control, copy gets no reading.
  • (B) Learn the case subspace on the original model, then patch it in the fine-tune. Runs: distributed alignment search, a learned rotation trained so that swapping its coordinates between a capitalised and a lowercase input swaps the original model's own case-dependent prediction; then the same swap in the fine-tune. Stronger than one direction, still fitted on the original model. Tells you whether some case subspace of the original model carries the fine-tune's answer. Next: if yes, case is used beyond the one readout; if no, the fine-tune likely reads the word itself, or case nonlinearly. Cost: $0, about a day. Limit: a learned subspace departs from Jain's linear readout and is stated as one.
  • (C) Build the second pair anyway. Runs: subject number (the label depends on whether the clause's subject is singular or plural, twin from shuffled nouns), same checks. Tells you whether the miss is specific to case. Next: a hit makes case the odd task; a miss brings you back here. Cost: $0 of compute, more code than A or B. Limit: a second miss adds little without A or B.
  • (D) Stop the definition line here. Runs nothing; the current reading is written up as it stands and the line closes. Limit: leaves "check or task?" open, with no outside reuse label to score decompositions against.

Recommendation: A. It is the only option that tells apart "the check is too strict" from "the case fine-tune really doesn't use the readout". B is the natural follow-up if copy also reads "not through".

The slide's new "pass" for a rank-8 LoRA under the least-squares split rests on one attacker start and clears a line by under 1%. Re-read it from more starts?

The question. The least-squares split moves each subcomponent by the smallest change that reproduces a fine-tune to first order and leaves the rest of the update in Δ, the weights outside the subcomponents. The slide reads it with the adversarial check: an attacker searches over masks of the subcomponents, and its damage is divided by its damage on the unchanged model (1.40 or less passes, 1.61 or more fails). Those readings used one attacker start. This is the 67M model, where work runs only when you ask by name.

The evidence. On the lowercase task the rank-8 LoRA's least-squares state reads 1.23 and the Adam fine-tune's 12.3; we predicted both would fail. The pass line measured for these rows is 1.241, which 1.23 clears by under 1%, and a similar trained decomposition moved from 1.23 to 1.33 across two starts. The state leaves 38% of the update in Δ whatever the check reads. The part the moved subcomponents can express keeps 0.93 of the gain and reads 1.14 on the LoRA; Adam fails the same read. Why is unknown.

Options. (a) Leave the slide as it is, with the one start said beside the row; nothing runs. (b, recommended) Two free cluster jobs of about 26 and 23 minutes (estimated): the first reads the LoRA's do-nothing, least-squares and expressible states at two more starts and on 256 more rows; the second reads Adam's four states at two more starts, plus its least-squares state shrunk to the LoRA's size and cut to rank 8. If the LoRA stays under the line the slide stands; if it rises, the cell becomes "between" or "fail".

Recommendation: b. It costs no money, and a move as small as one already seen across starts would take this state over 1.241, though not the slide's 1.40.
