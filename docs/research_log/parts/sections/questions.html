What we are asking

The question and its sub-questions

The central question · in the user's words, 2026-10-14

Given a VPD parameter decomposition, can one commute small gradient updates from post-training past the parameter components?

If so, the decomposition stays interpretable continuously along training, within some limit. If not, is that because post-training teaches new capabilities rather than strengthening existing ones, or because parameter decompositions are too brittle?

Precisely: is there a simple operation that maps a post-training update, say a LoRA, to an update on the components, such that the result is a faithful decomposition of the new model?

Q1 is that question; the others break it down. The three being worked on come first: Q1 and Q5, the two parallel lines (section {{n:next}}), and Q11, whether the optimiser matters, a priority subgoal. The rest follow in number order.

Q1
Is there a simple map that keeps the decomposition faithful to the updated model, with Δ not absorbing the update? The weak version; the pass criterion. The original question, open; one of two parallel lines since 2026-10-22 (user).
Partly. Component training with Adam, the one optimiser in the scored runs, keeps the decomposition faithful on about half the tasks, emoticon included to step 1000 (the Overview's figure; E16); comparing optimisers, those verdicts depend on the rate and the step. Adam's failures came while it was still damaging the model elsewhere: at steps 100 and 1,000 the same rate passes two of the failing word tasks, and a lower rate passes the 32-pair cipher. SGD fails both word tasks at step 1,000, and the rare-target task " in" then " Fig" fails under every arm (E21). A second batch order reproduces both on " it" then " is" and " which" then " was": Adam passes at steps 100 and 1,000 (0.95 to 1.14), SGD fails at step 1,000 (3.06, 2.91) (E26). The 2026-10-21 move-back condition this met is retired: the two lines now run in parallel. No split of a finished update keeps the behaviour, faithfulness and Δ fixed together (E16, first figure), and nor does splitting each step of the fine-tune (E19). Those adversarial failures are the large Adam update's: two small fine-tunes, SGD 0.01 and Adam 3e-6, pass under do-nothing, least squares and adjoint at steps 10, 100 and 1,000, the SGD one at step 1,000 past the large update's size (E22). Least squares still leaves 0.25 to 0.39 of the change in Δ; SGD's adjoint split keeps about 0.6 of the target probability at step 1,000 with Δ fixed on two emoticon seeds, the closest any frozen-Γ placement of a fine-tune has come, but it keeps less as the SGD update grows, at a level that depends on the task (E22, E31). The step split (E25) and a plain-SGD LoRA (E27) also pass at every step read. Leaving the subcomponents frozen and shifting Γ's values by one fixed change everywhere keeps the trigger behaviour but fails the adversary on ordinary text (E30). Teacher training, component training aimed at a finished fine-tune's predictions with Γ and Δ frozen, keeps the behaviour, faithfulness and Δ together for six fine-tunes on three tasks, from the task's text or from ordinary text alone, even with the emoticon trigger removed from that text (E34): the closest answer yet, one seed and a 20-step adversary. Letting Γ see the updated model is being tried (E15). The pass line itself is in question: the do-nothing reference fails it for the large Adam fine-tune at step 10 (section {{n:gaps}}; pending decision).

Q5
When a fine-tune changes behaviour, is it because Γ switches existing subcomponents on and off differently (re-gating)? If so, what is that switching, and can it be trusted? One of the two parallel lines. About mechanism, not the pass criterion.
On tasks the model already does, most of a fine-tune's gain comes from Γ switching existing subcomponents on, not from the subcomponents changing; on tasks it lacks, subcomponents Γ keeps off carry it (E5, E12, E16). The switching looks like a real mechanism rather than a leak: one fixed shift to Γ's importance values, the same for every input, reproduces most of it (E18, E24). That is the fixed-shift hypothesis. One change for all inputs works on seven tasks the model already does and fails on the copy task and the ciphers (E28). A separate fixed change for each cue word works on every task tested, the copy task included, and cues that share an answer share about half of it (E32, E33). Since 2026-10-23 the hypothesis reads "one fixed change per cue", tentatively; "cue" is defined only as the trigger token, so the next step is a fine-tune with no single trigger, to define the hypothesis without cues.

Q11
Does the optimiser matter? SGD and Adam on the same tasks, both as component training's optimiser and as the fine-tune optimiser whose update the splits consume. A priority subgoal since 2026-10-21 (user). Its runs are E21, analysed 2026-10-22, with follow-ups E22, E23, E26 and E27.
Neither the optimiser nor the size decides alone (E21). A small fine-tune passes the adversarial check under do-nothing and the least-squares split whether SGD or Adam made it (1.12 and 1.16 for SGD, 1.08 and 1.12 for Adam at a matched size), so on the fine-tune side the size of the change matters, not the optimiser (E21, third figure). For component training on the word tasks, step 10 favours SGD: it passes " it" then " is" and " which" then " was" where Adam at its usual rate fails. By steps 100 and 1,000 that reverses: the same Adam rate passes both, and SGD fails both at step 1,000 (2.82, 3.00) at about Adam's size of change (E21, first figure). Within each optimiser one quantity nearly sorts the verdicts, chosen after looking: size of change for SGD, damage elsewhere for Adam, whose fails all came while it damaged the model's other predictions by 0.2 nats or more (E21, second figure). On " in" then " Fig" nothing passes cleanly. On the cipher, E10's 32-pair failure goes away at a lower Adam rate. SGD fails the identity control at a smaller change than Adam passes with, so size does not explain the cipher. Matched for size on emoticon, Adam's change is about seven times more diffuse than SGD's. A second batch order reproduces the r0 and r1 verdicts for both optimisers (E26). Along 1,000 steps both small fine-tunes keep passing; the outside-both share's rise is Adam's at both sizes (0.38 small, 0.40 large at step 1,000), while SGD's stays at 0.03 to 0.06 of a random update's (E22). For a LoRA the optimiser sets the level, not the reading: a plain-SGD LoRA's read factor stays at its start and its outside share reads 0.33 to 0.87 of the null, against Adam's 0.54 to 1.07 (E27). Every verdict reads the 20-step adversary; under the paper's step rule the verdicts tried hold to 160 steps (E20, third amendment), and E21's step-1,000 states too: SGD stays 2.2 to 2.6 times the original model's damage, Adam 0.98 to 1.03 (E23). On the fine-tune side, the adjoint split keeps more of a small SGD update's behaviour than of a small Adam one's on emoticon, on two seeds; swapping the rates between tasks shows the SGD split keeping less the larger the update within each task, with the tasks still apart at one size, while Adam updates do not follow the SGD curves (E31, amendment).

Q2
Which operation carries an update onto the subcomponents and keeps the decomposition faithful: the rescaling split, the least-squares or adjoint split of a finished update, or component training?
Component training with Adam, on the emoticon task: the only one that keeps the behaviour and faithfulness with Δ untouched, to step 1000 (E16, first figure, all three panels). The rescaling split keeps none of the behaviour; the least-squares split puts a large part of the update in Δ and, for the fine-tune, fails the adversarial check; the adjoint split loses the behaviour as training goes on. Small fine-tunes to step 1,000 read the same way: least squares passes the adversary but leaves 0.25 to 0.39 in Δ, and the adjoint split loses Adam's behaviour, though it keeps about 0.6 of SGD's target probability on two emoticon seeds (E22, E31); for SGD it keeps less the larger the update, at a level that depends on the task (E31, amendment).

Q3
Is a LoRA easier to commute than a full-weight fine-tune?
Not on alignment: a LoRA's read factor barely moves from its random start (E9), so it lines up with what the subcomponents read about as well as a random update, while the fine-tune lines up better (E14's figure, left panel). On the adversarial check, yes: its least-squares split stays under the 1.5 line at all three steps, where the fine-tune's fails at steps 10 and 1000 (E16, first figure, panel a). But that split leaves more of the LoRA than of the fine-tune in Δ (panel c). The LoRA's form, not Adam, sets its outside share: trained with plain SGD its read factor keeps a mean cosine of 0.997 with its start, and every placement passes (E27).

Q4
Does it matter whether the behaviour is new to the model? The "new capabilities" branch.
Partly. Component training learns a cipher the model has never seen and keeps the decomposition faithful for one new pair, for the identity control and, once training settles, for 32 pairs. The 32-pair failure belonged to the step it was read at, mid-learning with its damage elsewhere at its peak, not to the number of new associations or the update's size (E10, second figure). Behaviours the model has and behaviours it lacks take different routes through the decomposition (E12, figure). A lower Adam rate also passes the 32 pairs, and SGD fails the identity control at a smaller size than Adam passes with (E21). So for component training, novelty has not been seen to break faithfulness. For re-gating it does: one fixed change carries Γ's switching on seven tasks the model already does and on none of three key-value tasks it lacks (E28).

Q6
Why does the adversarial check fail where the stochastic one does not? Is the decomposition brittle?
Not because the changes cancel each other (E17), and not because the original subcomponents carry the damage (E20). The adversary's extra damage comes from removing alive and dead subcomponents together: at the original model those two removals offset each other, after a large update they no longer do (E20). SGD's failures have the same shape (E23), and the verdicts do not depend on how long the adversary searches (E20). Why the offset is lost is open.

Q8
Should the map act on each training step rather than on a finished run?
Raised 2026-10-19, undecided. Only component training is per-step by construction; every split so far was one-shot on a finished update, at steps from 1 to 1000 (E2, E9, E16). The first per-step split with moving pieces, the step split, ran as E19: it keeps the weights exact but leaves more of the behaviour in Δ than the least-squares split of the finished update. The pieces' movement made no difference; refitting the split's scale every step pushed more of the update into Δ. Answered no for the adjoint form, on one task. Along two small fine-tunes the step split passes the adversary at every step (0.98 and 1.02 at step 1,000, against E19's 2.67), and still leaves more behaviour in Δ than least squares except on SGD at step 1,000 (E25).
