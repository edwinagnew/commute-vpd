What we are asking

2The question and its sub-questions

In the user's words (2026-10-14): given a VPD parameter decomposition, can one commute small gradient updates from post-training past the parameter components? If so, this gives continuous interpretability within some limit. If not, is it because post-training teaches new capabilities rather than strengthening existing ones, or because parameter decompositions are too brittle? Precisely: is there a simple operation that maps a post-training update, say a LoRA, to an update on the components, such that the result is a faithful decomposition of the new model?

Since 2026-10-21 the main sub-question is Q5, placed first. The others keep their numbers; Q1 is the original question and stays open. Q11, comparing SGD and Adam component training, was made a priority subgoal by the user the same day; its analysis is in (E21). Every adversarial verdict below reads VPD's 20-step adversary; under the VPD paper's step rule each verdict tried reads the same at 160 steps (E20, third amendment).

Q5
What is re-gating, and can it be trusted? Where does an update go: strengthened pieces, re-switched existing ones, switched-off ones, or Δ? The main sub-question since 2026-10-21. Mechanism, not the commute criterion.
Under every operation route-split so far, the moved subcomponents carry about a tenth of the gain or less (E5's figure, E16's second figure). Those states came from Adam component training or a finished update; the one SGD state route-split since agrees, its moved subcomponents carrying 0.07 of the gain (E21). For common next words and copying, Γ switching existing subcomponents on carries most of it ("reveal"); for a new association, subcomponents Γ keeps off do ("create"); emoticon and the rarest next words sit between (E5's figure). See E12, whose figure is in the Overview. Mechanism or leak, the first control, ran as E18. One fixed pattern of importance values, the same for every input, gives back most of the switching effect on all three tasks, and random or wrong-target patterns give back none, so no leak is seen (E18, first figure). The filed test, a fixed change added to each input's own values, cleared its bar on emoticon only. On the two word tasks it landed between the mechanism and leak bars, a band the plan's decision tree did not interpret (E18, second figure). An amendment traced that miss to how a fixed change meets each input's own original values, not to information about the input: a fixed change recovers at least half of the effect on every task, and 0.70 to 0.86 on averaged values (E18, fifth figure). The user did not back that as a mechanism: it is the leading hypothesis, H, in three parts. One change to the importance values is (a) the same for every input, (b) specific to which subcomponents receive it, and (c) enough on its own. Two more amendments tested each part. Shuffled among the subcomponents, the change recovers nothing, and another window's updated values do 0.89 to 1.00 as well as the window's own, so (a) and (b) survive. Part (c) failed as filed: the input-varying remainder alone recovers 0.16 to 0.31 (E18, seventh figure), and stays failed. A fourth amendment found what that remainder is: another input's remainder recovers 0.82 to 0.96 as much, close to the original model's averaged values, so it is mostly an averaging baseline the bar did not account for, and 0.01 to 0.05 of the effect is input-specific. Since 2026-10-22 the user holds a restated H as a working hypothesis, not a finding: one fixed change carries most of the switching effect, and inputs' own variation adds a few hundredths. A surprise: the subcomponents the update turns down carry about as much of the recovery as the switched-on set (E18, sixth figure).

Q1
Is there a simple map that keeps the decomposition faithful to the updated model, with Δ not absorbing the update? The weak version; the pass criterion. The original question; second since 2026-10-21, open.
Partly. Component training with Adam, the one optimiser in the scored runs, keeps the decomposition faithful on about half the tasks, emoticon included to step 1000 (the Overview's figure; E16); comparing optimisers, those verdicts depend on the rate and the step. Adam's failures came while it was still damaging the model elsewhere: at steps 100 and 1,000 the same rate passes two of the failing word tasks, and a lower rate passes the 32-pair cipher. SGD fails both word tasks at step 1,000, and the rare-target task " in" then " Fig" fails under every arm (E21). Adam component training thus meets the main line's rule for moving back on emoticon, " it" then " is" and " which" then " was"; the decision is the user's. No split of a finished update keeps the behaviour, faithfulness and Δ fixed together (E16, first figure), and nor does splitting each step of the fine-tune (E19). Letting Γ see the updated model is being tried (E15). The pass line itself is in question: the do-nothing reference fails it for fine-tunes (section 9).

Q11
Does the optimiser matter? SGD and Adam on the same tasks, both as component training's optimiser and as the fine-tune optimiser whose update the splits consume. A priority subgoal since 2026-10-21 (user). Its runs are E21, analysed 2026-10-22.
Neither the optimiser nor the size decides alone (E21). A small fine-tune passes the adversarial check under do-nothing and the least-squares split whether SGD or Adam made it (1.12 and 1.16 for SGD, 1.08 and 1.12 for Adam at a matched size), so on the fine-tune side the size of the change matters, not the optimiser (E21, third figure). For component training on the word tasks, step 10 favours SGD: it passes " it" then " is" and " which" then " was" where Adam at its usual rate fails. By steps 100 and 1,000 that reverses: the same Adam rate passes both, and SGD fails both at step 1,000 (2.82, 3.00) at about Adam's size of change (E21, first figure). Within each optimiser one quantity nearly sorts the verdicts, chosen after looking: size of change for SGD, damage elsewhere for Adam, whose fails all came while it damaged the model's other predictions by 0.2 nats or more (E21, second figure). On " in" then " Fig" nothing passes cleanly. On the cipher, E10's 32-pair failure goes away at a lower Adam rate. SGD fails the identity control at a smaller change than Adam passes with, so size does not explain the cipher. Matched for size on emoticon, Adam's change is about seven times more diffuse than SGD's. One seed throughout. Every verdict reads the 20-step adversary; under the paper's step rule the verdicts tried hold to 160 steps (E20, third amendment), though E21's own states were not rerun.

Q2
Which operation carries an update onto the subcomponents and keeps the decomposition faithful: the rescaling split, the least-squares or adjoint split of a finished update, or component training?
Component training with Adam, on the emoticon task: the only one that keeps the behaviour and faithfulness with Δ untouched, to step 1000 (E16, first figure, all three panels). The rescaling split keeps none of the behaviour; the least-squares split puts a large part of the update in Δ and, for the fine-tune, fails the adversarial check; the adjoint split loses the behaviour as training goes on. One task, one seed.

Q3
Is a LoRA easier to commute than a full-weight fine-tune?
Not on alignment: a LoRA's read factor barely moves from its random start (E9), so it lines up with what the subcomponents read about as well as a random update, while the fine-tune lines up better (E14's figure, left panel). On the adversarial check, yes: its least-squares split stays under the 1.5 line at all three steps, where the fine-tune's fails at steps 10 and 1000 (E16, first figure, panel a). But that split leaves more of the LoRA than of the fine-tune in Δ (panel c).

Q4
Does it matter whether the behaviour is new to the model? The "new capabilities" branch.
Partly. Component training learns a cipher the model has never seen and stays faithful for one new pair but not for 32 (E10's figure, second panel). Behaviours the model has and behaviours it lacks take different routes through the decomposition (E12, figure). That 32-pair failure was Adam's rate: at a lower rate Adam learns the 32 pairs and passes. SGD fails the 32 pairs and the identity control, the latter at a smaller size of change than Adam passes with, so size does not explain it either (E21). Whether novelty does is untested.

Q6
Why does the adversarial check fail where the stochastic one does not? Is the decomposition brittle?
Not because the changes cancel. Component training's changes add coherently on every task and a random half of them costs about a quarter of the full revert (E17, first figure); removing exactly the part of the changes the adversary removes costs 1 to 17% of its extra damage, the same as a random pattern (E17, second figure). The adversary needs a selective mask (A1). Nor is the rest of its damage in the original subcomponents, as E17 had inferred: removing the adversary's share of them, changes kept, costs no more than at the original model on three of four failing states, and a fifth of the excess on the fourth (E20, first figure). E20's amendment split the rest: Δ alone is negligible and the dead subcomponents alone cost no more after the update. The excess is the interaction between the adversary's removal of the alive subcomponents and of the dead ones, which offset each other at the original model and much less after the update (E20, fourth figure). A second amendment ruled out the same mask costing more: the original model's own mask costs about the same at every updated model, so each failing model's search finds a different mask. Its runaway climb with steps (0.66 to 14 nats by 100) came from a fixed step of 0.1; with the VPD paper's step rule a third amendment found 0.66 to 0.71 nats from 20 to 160 steps, and every failing state's ratio within a tenth, so the fails are not an artefact of the budget. These failing states are the large updates; three of the four pass at a smaller or later update (E21), so what Q6 explains is the large update's fragility, and r2 t275's.

Q8
Should the map act on each training step rather than on a finished run?
Raised 2026-10-19, undecided. Only component training is per-step by construction; every split so far was one-shot on a finished update, at steps from 1 to 1000 (E2, E9, E16). The first per-step split with moving pieces, the step split, ran as E19: it keeps the weights exact but leaves more of the behaviour in Δ than the least-squares split of the finished update. The pieces' movement made no difference; refitting the split's scale every step pushed more of the update into Δ. Answered no for the adjoint form, on one task.
