Does the fine-tuned model itself use the subcomponents Γ switches on?

2026-10-27 to 2026-10-28 · RunPod RTX 4090, MATS cluster L40 · experiments/recruitment_test

Partly: putting the switched-on set's trigger outputs back to the original model's values takes back about a fifth of the fine-tune's gain on two of three tasks, where sets whose outputs moved most take back none; but as many subcomponents picked for pointing toward the target take back as much or more, so Γ's set does not stand out against them, though Γ finds many of them without being told the target.

Run
A 10-step fine-tune teaches the 67M model to follow a trigger with a target token (E18's tasks: emoticon, ` which`, ` we`; two seeds each; a fine-tune to ` http` at the same triggers is the null). With Γ frozen, a set of the original model's subcomponents is switched on at the trigger: the switched-on set S, the alive subcomponents whose importance there rises by more than 0.3 (21 to 213 of them). Every number is a share of the gain: the fine-tune raises the target's log-odds at the trigger by 10 to 14 nats, and a share is how much of that rise an intervention undoes, 0 none and 1 all, on held-out windows. Four steps, one per figure. (1) Remove S from the weights (RunPod, about $0.23). (2) Census what S does in Pile text. (3) Put back only S's trigger outputs to their original-model values, beside random, null and already-active sets, and beside the subcomponents outside S whose outputs changed most. (4) Do the same for the subcomponents outside S whose changes pull hardest on the target to first order (this ranking reads the target and no Γ), as many as S has in each matrix. Steps 2 to 4 ran on the cluster, $0 (step 4: job 40121). Controls ran first each time; steps 3 and 4 were checked by an independent agent.
Found
  • S is mostly the original model's own machinery for the target (60 to 96% of it is followed by the target in Pile text, against 2 to 8% of random sets), and removing it already costs the original model, so removal cannot say how much of the fine-tune runs through it.
  • Putting back S's trigger outputs takes back about a fifth of the gain on emoticon and ` which` (under 0.05 on ` we`); random sets, the null's set, already-active sets and the subcomponents whose outputs moved most take back about none, so the size of a change does not explain S's share.
  • Checked claim: ranked by first-order pull on the target and matched to S's count in each matrix, a set takes back more of the gain than S on emoticon and about the same on ` which`, so S does not take back more than target-pointing subcomponents do. On emoticon the gap comes mostly from members of S that push against the target. S, found without the target, still holds about ten times the chance share of the most target-pointing subcomponents.
