Does a LoRA or a fine-tune line up with what the subcomponents read and write?

2026-10-20 · RunPod RTX A5000 · experiments/lora_split

The question. The commute line asks whether a finished update can be carried onto the decomposition's pieces. Q3 asks whether a LoRA is easier to carry than a full fine-tune. Each of the 24 decomposed weight matrices is a sum of rank-one subcomponents: each reads its input along one direction and writes its output along another. An update the subcomponents could produce must read inputs they read and write outputs they write. Earlier runs, up to E9, gave one number that mixed both sides. By it a LoRA sat outside the subcomponents about as much as a random update, and a fine-tune well inside. This run asks which side each update misses.

What we did. We took two updates on the emoticon task, where the 67M model learns to answer a trigger with the letter o. One is a full fine-tune of all 24 matrices with Adam at rate 3e-5. The other is a rank-8 LoRA on all 24. Both are on one seed and read at nine steps from 1 to 1,000. For each we measured how much of its effect on real Pile inputs lies on what the subcomponents read (the read side) and on what they write (the write side). Each share is divided by the exact share for a random update with the same size in each matrix, so 1 means no better lined up than random. These two are pooled over the 20 matrices where the subcomponents do not already cover a whole side, since there every update reads 1. We also measured the rescaling share: how much of the update a change of the existing subcomponents' sizes alone explains. Last, at steps 10, 100 and 1,000, we ran the adversarial check in two ways. Do-nothing leaves the update whole in Δ, the part of each matrix the subcomponents do not cover. The least-squares split divides it among the subcomponents. The check searches for the mask of subcomponents that does the most damage on Pile text, over the damage on the original decomposition. It passes below 1.37: one plus three times the original's relative spread over three adversary seeds, 0.12.

What we found. The fine-tune lines up on the read side from its first step, at about 1.3 times random, while the LoRA reads 0.97 to 1.16 at eight of nine steps and 1.32 at step 100 (first figure). On the write side neither is inside through step 30. After that the fine-tune rises to 1.10 at step 300 and 1.34 at step 1,000, and the LoRA falls below random (second figure). The fine-tune was predicted to be at least 1.3 on both sides at step 10, so the write side reversed. The share of the fine-tune a rescaling explains grows from 2% to 7% of its effect, which is 2 and then 9.6 times a random update's share (fourth figure). On the adversarial check this fine-tune fails at most steps, left in Δ or split. The LoRA passes at every step (fifth figure).

What it means. Through step 300 the fine-tune's lead over the LoRA is on the read side, which the data shapes; only at step 1,000 does it also write inside. A LoRA's read factor barely leaves its random start (E9). Why a gradient's outputs avoid what the subcomponents write is not explained. The contrast on the adversarial check belongs to this large update: in E21 and E22 a fine-tune 4.7 times smaller passes the check, left in Δ or split, at steps 10, 100 and 1,000. The rescaling climb also belongs to the large update alone (E22). E27 traced the LoRA's distance from the subcomponents to its form.

Run
The LoRAs were not retrained: E9's 24-matrix LoRA and E7's one-matrix LoRAs were read back from saved factors, beside one fresh emoticon fine-tune. One RunPod RTX A5000 run, 85 minutes plus a 13-minute smoke, $0.73.
Found
  • The fine-tune lines up with what the subcomponents read, about 1.3 times random from step 1; the LoRA reads lower, 0.97 to 1.16 at eight of nine steps.
  • Unexpectedly, neither update writes where the subcomponents write through step 30, and the fine-tune reaches 1.34 only at step 1,000, on one seed.
  • On one matrix, a LoRA's rank from 1 to 32 changes its read side by under 0.06; by step 1,000 a rescaling explains 7% of the fine-tune, 9.6 times a random update's share.
