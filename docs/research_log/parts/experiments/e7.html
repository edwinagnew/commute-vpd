Is a LoRA easier to carry onto the pieces than a full fine-tune, and does its rank matter?

2026-10-19 · RunPod RTX 4090 · experiments/lora_commute

The question. To read an update in the decomposition, its weight change has to be carried onto the pieces without breaking them (Q1). The working guess was that a LoRA, a fine-tune restricted to a low-rank change of one weight matrix, would be easier to carry than a full fine-tune of all 24 matrices (Q3), and that the difficulty would change with the LoRA's rank as well.

What we did. On the emoticon task, we trained LoRAs of rank 1, 4, 8 and 32 on the matrix the VPD paper studies, one MLP output matrix in layer 2, and a full fine-tune for comparison. Each update was carried onto the pieces two ways. Do-nothing leaves the whole change in Δ, the residual the pieces don't cover. The least-squares split writes it as the smallest change of the pieces that reproduces it, leaving only what they cannot express in Δ. A third map, component training, skips the update and trains the pieces on the task directly, on that one matrix or on all 24. Every state was scored on the adversarial ratio: the damage an adversary searching over masks of the pieces does on Pile text, over what it does to the unchanged decomposition. Under 1.11, three times the unchanged decomposition's seed-to-seed spread, it passes; over 1.5 it fails; in between is unresolved.

What we found. Rank made no clear difference on one seed, except that rank 8 was the worst of each kind on the adversarial ratio; the matrix mattered more. The LoRA's change is no more readable by the pieces than a random change of that matrix (second figure), so the split leaves a third or more of its gain in Δ, against a twelfth for the fine-tune (third figure). On that one matrix every state sits over the pass line: the split LoRAs read 1.35 to 2.02, failing only at rank 8, and rank-limited component training there fails at every rank (first figure). Component training across all 24 matrices sits just inside the pass line, at 1.10.

What it means. The run compared a LoRA on a matrix the decomposition reads poorly with a fine-tune spread over matrices it reads well, so it cannot say whether LoRAs as such are harder to carry; the chip is "no answer" for that reason. A later run, E8, put the LoRA on all 24 matrices and found its split passed where the fine-tune's failed; on E8's result, not this card's, the "harder" here is about the matrix, not about LoRAs. All numbers are from one seed per state.

Run
LoRAs of rank 1 to 32 on the paper's layer-2 MLP output matrix and a full fine-tune, emoticon task, one seed each, each carried by do-nothing and the least-squares split, beside component training on that matrix and on all 24. One RunPod RTX 4090 job of 37 minutes, $1.51 with its smoke run.
Found
  • On this matrix, rank made no clear difference on one seed per rank; rank 8 is the exception on the adversarial ratio (2.02 for its split, 4.06 for its component training), with nothing to say whether that is rank or noise.
  • The LoRA's change is as unreadable by the pieces as a random one, so its split leaves 0.33 to 0.43 of the gain in Δ against 0.09 for the fine-tune's, the opposite of the guess, though the fine-tune was spread over 24 matrices and the LoRA confined to one.
  • Confined to the one matrix, every map sits over the pass line and rank-limited component training fails; across all 24 matrices it passes.
