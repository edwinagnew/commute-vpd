Which simpler methods did we compare the share with, and how do they do on the same pairs?

2026-11-10 · provisional, no independent check · no run, saved results only, $0 · experiments/method_table

The answer. We built nine pairs of fine-tunes. One side of each pair reuses something the model already does; the other side is new. The re-gating share reads higher on the reuse side on all nine pairs. No simpler method does that. Steering agrees on five pairs and points the other way on three. Neurons agree on at most three. Update size agrees on none. Pairs are labelled as built; this is not a verdict on reuse.

The pairs. Each built pair is two sets of fine-tunes of the 67M model, with two to eight seeds a side. The reuse side does something the model half does already, such as copying a token. The new side learns something unfamiliar, such as a cipher. Below the line are four twin pairs: a real fine-tune against one trained on shuffled labels, which can only memorise.

The methods. Each simpler method tests one cheap explanation of what the share picks up.

MethodWhat it doesThe simpler explanation it tests
re-gating shareKeeps the original weights and only changes which subcomponents are switched on.The method under test.
share on untrained decompositionThe same reading, on each matrix's plain SVD instead of the trained decomposition.The signal comes from training the decomposition.
neuron readings (three forms)The same idea on MLP neurons: keep the original weights, use the fine-tune's neuron firing.Neurons would do as well.
steeringAdds the fine-tune's average activation change to the original model.One direction in activations would do as well.
priorHow likely the original model already found the trained answer.The share only tracks how familiar the answer was.
update sizeHow big the weight change is.The share only tracks how big the update was.
effective rankHow many directions the weight change spreads over.The share only tracks how spread out the update was.

What stands out.

  • The prior is the closest rival. It points the same way as the share on every pair it was read on. But on two pairs the prior is nearly equal on both sides, and the share still separates them.
  • Size and rank do not explain the share. On three pairs both sides have about the same update size, and the share still separates all three. Where size does differ, the new side has the bigger update.
  • Steering points the wrong way on three pairs. We have not worked out why.
  • Neurons and the untrained decomposition read small or mixed gaps. Without its training, the decomposition loses most of the share's gap.
  • Every rival fails somewhere, but no single pair rules out all of them at once. The prior plus steering together could in principle track the share. We have not tested that.

The twin pairs are less clear. The share separates only one of the four, half-known against its shuffle. On the two label pairs, steering, size and rank read the twin higher. That may reflect how the twins were trained (one trained ten times longer) more than what they learned.
