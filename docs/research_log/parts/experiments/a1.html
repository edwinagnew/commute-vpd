Why does the adversary break updated decompositions that the random-mask check passes?

2026-10-20 · from existing numbers · reports/analyses/2026-10-20_analysis_adversarial-vs-stochastic.md

The question. VPD writes each weight matrix as rank-one subcomponents plus a residual Δ, and Γ rates each subcomponent's importance on each input, from 0 to 1. VPD checks a decomposition by masking subcomponents and measuring the damage, the KL from the model to the masked copy on Pile text. E11 updated the decomposition by component training, 10 Adam steps on the subcomponents themselves, on five next-word tasks, each raising one word after a trigger, such as " was" after " which". It failed the adversarial check on three tasks while the random-mask check barely moved. Q6 asks why the two checks disagree.

What we did. No new run: this note read the measurement code and E11's saved scores together. A mask turns a subcomponent down by scaling it by a value between 1 and its importance, so a subcomponent of importance 1 cannot be touched. The random-mask check averages two random draws of the mask and, at each position, masks a random subset of the 24 matrices, about half on average. The adversary masks all 24 at once and searches for 20 steps for the mask that does the most damage. The adversarial ratio is its damage on the updated decomposition over its damage on the unchanged one; above 1.5 counts as a fail, the line set in E2. The unchanged decomposition's adversary damage is 0.70 nats, so a ratio is 0.70 plus the extra damage, over 0.70. A third, fixed mask turns every subcomponent down to its importance together, the all-down mask. For each mask the extra damage is its damage on the updated decomposition minus on the unchanged one.

What we found. On every task the adversary's mask does many times the extra damage of the all-down mask (figure): 50 times on " which" then " was" (0.87 against 0.017 nats) and 300 times on " it" then " is". On the other three tasks the factor is 7 to 10. This points to a selective mask, some subcomponents turned down and others left whole: the one uniform mask measured, all down together, nearly preserves the model, though no other non-selective mask was tried. The random-mask check adds under 0.01 nats on four tasks and 0.18 on " in" then " Fig" (figure). From the code, the note reads this as dilution: it masks only part of the model at random, so it would rarely hit one specific joint pattern; that was not tested.

What it means. This note read the gap as cancellation: the update spread over many subcomponents whose changes oppose each other and add up correctly only when all are present. That was this note's reading, not a measurement; nothing here tests it. E17 then tested it on six component-training states and two splits and refuted it, on a test that had no working positive control: a state built to cancel did not fail either. E20 found the damage was not on the original subcomponents either, on three of four failing states, but in how removing the alive subcomponents (those Γ rates important somewhere) and the dead ones combine.
