When the pieces learn the emoticon behaviour, do a few of them carry it, and do they stay the same pieces?

2026-10-15 · RunPod RTX 4090 · experiments/identity_test

The question. VPD writes each weight matrix of the 67M model as a sum of rank-one pieces (subcomponents); 9,881 of them are alive, that is, ever switched on. Q5 asks what the decomposition says about how the model changed when it is fine-tuned. The hopeful reading is that a fine-tune strengthens a few pieces the model already has and leaves them recognisably themselves. E2 had just shown that component training, which trains the pieces' own weights on the task instead of the model's, learns the behaviour and stays faithful at 10 and 30 steps. This experiment asks where in those pieces the new behaviour sits.

What we did. The task is VPD's hand-editing example, the emoticon task: after the first character of an emoticon, predict o. The gain is how much the fine-tune raised the log-odds of o there, on 73 held-out windows: 11.6 after 30 steps of component training with Adam. We put pieces back to their original weights, first one at a time to rank them, then the top k together, and measured how much of the gain each removal undid. Effects are not simply added up: each top-k group is put back at once and measured. We also asked whether the 20 pieces that mattered most were still the same pieces: a piece passes the identity test if what it reads still correlates at 0.9 or more with its old self, it is still its own best match among its matrix's pieces, and what it writes is as close to its old self as two halves of its own data are to each other, within 0.1. We also looked at the piece the VPD paper itself edits for this behaviour, number 2359 of layer 2's MLP output, with its five siblings. For comparison, we took the same measures on an ordinary fine-tune of the same size, carried onto the pieces by the least-squares split. That split divides the finished weight change among the pieces with the smallest possible change to each and leaves the rest in the residual Δ.

What we found. The gain is spread over thousands of pieces. That reading rests on putting pieces back, and their effects interact: groups are measured together, and a group undoes a different amount than its members' single effects add up to. Putting back the ten that matter most undoes 1.6% of it, and the top 1,000 undo 36%, at 10 and at 30 steps alike (first figure); no single piece undoes more than 0.03 of the gain of 11.6 (second figure). The paper's emoticon piece is not among the top ten. Its size ratio, the size of its rank-one weight after training over before, is 1.000: it was not strengthened. Every one of the 20 top pieces passed the identity test, but so did 20 pieces that barely mattered: 99% of all alive pieces moved by under 1% (third figure).

What it means. On this task, with Adam and one seed, component training learns the behaviour through small changes spread over thousands of pieces, not by strengthening a few, and not the one the decomposition labels as the emoticon piece. The identity question gets no answer: a test that passes for every piece, with no case built to make it fail, cannot tell us anything. The "no answer" chip comes from this void identity half; the concentration half does have an answer, that the gain is diffuse. The open caveat was whether the spread comes from Adam, which rescales every coordinate, or from the task, and E4 tested that next.

Run
E2's component-training states (Adam, steps 10 and 30) and least-squares splits of a fine-tune at matched sizes were regenerated on the emoticon task, one seed, and every alive piece was put back alone and in top-k groups. One RunPod RTX 4090 job, about 9 minutes and $0.35.
Found
  • On the emoticon task with Adam, the gain is diffuse, as predicted before the run: the top ten pieces undo 1.6% of it and the top 1,000 undo 36%, at both 10 and 30 steps.
  • The VPD paper's emoticon piece and its siblings are not among the top ten and are not strengthened (size ratio 1.000).
  • The identity test passed for every piece, including the low-effect ones, with changes under 1% and no positive control, so it is uninformative. At matched size, the least-squares split's top pieces carry about 2.5 times more than component training's, still diffuse.
