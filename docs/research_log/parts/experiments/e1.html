Does a small fine-tune land on the decomposition's existing pieces?

2026-10-14 · RunPod L40S · experiments/transport_test

The question. This was the project's first run, on the question Q2 asks: which operation carries a fine-tune's weight change onto the decomposition, so that the decomposition still describes the fine-tuned model? The decomposition is Goodfire's released parameter decomposition (VPD) of their 4-layer 67M model trained on the Pile. It writes each weight matrix as a sum of rank-one pieces (subcomponents), plus a leftover Δ (the part of each matrix the pieces do not cover), and a learned function Γ decides which pieces are switched on at each token. The simplest hope is that a small fine-tune only turns some existing pieces up or down. If so, the update is already described by pieces we can read.

What we did. We ran two short fine-tunes. The emoticon fine-tune teaches the model to predict "o" after emoticon openings such as " :", where the VPD paper's emoticon piece fires; the paper changed that behaviour by editing this one piece. Continued pretraining is more ordinary training on the Pile: a pure strengthening update, the case the main prediction was written for. We wrote each update with the 9,881 alive pieces, those Γ ever switches on, in three ways. The rescaling split keeps each piece's direction and picks its size by least squares, to come closest to the weight change. A refitted rescaling picks the sizes to reproduce the fine-tune's outputs instead. Free write edits let k pieces change their write vector, the output direction of the rank-one piece, freely: the k with the largest shares of the rescaling split, or k random ones. The share in the span is the fraction of the update's squared size that the rescaling split reproduces; it needs no training. Capture is the fraction of the fine-tune's change in next-token predictions that a map reproduces on held-out text: 1 is all, 0 none.

What we found. As weights, both updates lie almost wholly outside the existing pieces. Only 0.06% of the emoticon update and 0.05% of continued pretraining sit in their span, against 0.035% for a random direction (first figure). The rescaling split reproduces 0.1% of the emoticon behaviour (second figure). Ten edited pieces or a full refit reproduce all of it, but ten random pieces do as well as the ten chosen ones, so at that size the test cannot tell whether the decomposition knows where the update went. The paper's emoticon piece does rank second in its matrix at step 10 (third figure).

What it means. The literal rescaling of fixed pieces cannot read these updates: almost all of each update goes into Δ. Whether some other map onto the pieces can is left open, because the behaviour test had no power at ten pieces and the continued-pretraining fits failed to train (their learning rate was too large). The next experiment (E2) changed the map, the random baseline and the way pieces were chosen.

Run
One seed per fine-tune, read at steps 10, 30, 100 and 300; the emoticon fine-tune is scored at step 10, continued pretraining at step 100. One RunPod L40S, 114 minutes, about $2.1.
Found
  • On both fine-tunes and at every step, under 0.4% of the update lies among the existing pieces, and the report's tables show the rescaling split carrying under 0.4% of the behaviour at every step (only step 10 of the emoticon fine-tune is plotted). Continued pretraining was predicted to reach ten times random and reached 1.5 times.
  • On the emoticon fine-tune, ten random pieces with free write edits reproduce the behaviour as well as the ten chosen ones, so the behaviour test had no power at ten pieces. The continued-pretraining fits failed to train and say nothing, as the same-day audit found from their training losses (reports/reviews/2026-10-14_review_round1-audit.md).
  • The report (not plotted here) finds the decomposition's error under random masking of pieces stayed within 0.94 to 1.02 times its value on the original model, under every map. That check passes even for doing nothing, since whatever the pieces do not carry lands in Δ.

Moved on
The report and its same-day code audit found three design flaws, all fixed in E2's design: the faithfulness check let Δ absorb the update, so do-nothing passed it; the random baseline was too expressive; and ranking pieces by their share of the rescaling split picked the wrong ones. The emoticon update's climb from 1.7 to 10 times random between steps 10 and 300 is still unexplained.
