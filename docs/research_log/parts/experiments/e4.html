Can a handful of pieces learn the emoticon behaviour, and do those pieces then carry it?

2026-10-15, follow-up 2026-10-21 · RunPod RTX 4090, MATS cluster L40 · experiments/concentration_test, experiments/sgd_grid

The question. Component training trains the decomposition's pieces directly on a task, with Γ and Δ frozen. Earlier, with Adam and every piece free, it learned the emoticon behaviour but spread its change thinly over thousands of pieces. Can a handful of pieces learn it instead, including the six the VPD paper labels for emoticons, and do those pieces then carry it? That bears on the re-gating line, Q5: is an update explained by the pieces it changes, or by Γ switching on pieces that were already there? A side question: does plain SGD give a more concentrated change than Adam?

What we did. The decomposition writes each weight matrix as a sum of rank-one pieces plus a remainder Δ. Γ, a frozen network, reads the model's activations and gives each piece an importance value from 0 to 1 at each position: its switch. The task is emoticon: after the first character of an emoticon, predict o. We trained all 9,881 live pieces, or only the top 1, 10 or 100 by how fast the loss falls as each changes, or only the paper's six. Damage elsewhere is the KL from the original model on held-out text away from the trigger, with a limit of 0.110. The adversarial ratio is the damage an adversary choosing masks of the pieces does on Pile text, over what it does to the unchanged decomposition; near 1 passes. The stand-in runs the model on the pieces alone, each scaled by Γ's importance value, with Δ dropped. Its probability of the target says whether the pieces explain the behaviour. Do-nothing leaves every piece unchanged and puts the weight change in Δ, so in the stand-in it gains only through Γ reading changed activations. Two later reads held Γ's importance values at the original model's, and listed the pieces that switch on.

What we found. One, ten or the paper's six pieces each learn the behaviour with little damage elsewhere (first figure). In the stand-in they reach 0.98 at the trigger, but do-nothing, with no piece changed, already reaches 0.53, against 0.004 for the original model. With Γ's switches held at the original's, no arm's pieces carry the behaviour by the report's rule, which asks for 0.8 of the target: the six emoticon pieces keep 0.37, well below 0.98 but not zero, and the other arms shown at most 0.10 (second figure). Every update switches on about 150 to 200 pre-existing pieces, largely the same ones (report), which carry about half of that switching effect (third figure). Plain SGD seemed to fail at every rate, but E4's grid of rates started too high (fourth figure).

What it means. A sparse update learns the behaviour, but in the pieces-only model much of it comes through Γ switching existing pieces on, and less through the trained pieces themselves. That reading rests on one task, one seed and 30 steps, on a stand-in that drops Δ, and on a switched-on set that accounts for about half the switching effect (0.45 to 0.64). That was the project's first evidence for "reveal": the decomposition already holds machinery that an update recruits through Γ. Since re-switching makes up much of every stand-in number, the project next split each gain into the pieces' own change and re-switching, the route split, before scoring the task ladder. The caveat: the switched-on set carries only about half the effect, and what its pieces do elsewhere was not measured here.

Run
Component training on emoticon, one seed, 30 steps: every piece free (Adam; plain SGD at rates 0.3 to 30), the top 1, 10 or 100 pieces, or the paper's six, then two reads of the saved states. RunPod RTX 4090, about $1 in all; the SGD follow-up at rates 0.001 to 0.1 ran on a MATS cluster L40 for $0.
Found
  • As expected, one, ten or the paper's six pieces each learn the behaviour cleanly: damage elsewhere at most 0.020, adversarial ratio at most 1.08.
  • Against expectation, the stand-in reaches the target with much of it through Γ switching other pieces on: with the original switches, the six emoticon pieces keep 0.37 of the target, well below 0.98, and the rest at most 0.19 (report). A largely shared set of about 150 to 200 switched-on pieces (report) carries about half of that switching effect.
  • Plain SGD learns cleanly at rates 0.003 to 0.1, below E4's grid. Its update is still spread out: putting back the top ten pieces removes only 0.16 of the gain (report), measured at a smaller change than Adam's, so not compared with Adam here.
